Charts for defects and defectives

Some inspections end in a count instead of a measurement: scratches on a panel, chips that fail a probe test. This module builds control charts for counts and proportions from the Poisson and binomial models, and shows where their three-sigma limits mislead.

Defects and defectives

Many quality checks have no scale to read. An inspector looks over a painted car door and counts scratches, or a tester probes each chip on a wafer and marks it pass or fail. The handbook calls data like these attributes, because they come from counting or classifying. Attributes also make sense when a measurement exists but costs too much to take on every unit.

Attribute data come in two kinds. Mixing them up leads straight to the wrong chart.

Defect

Also called a nonconformity

One flaw: a scratch, a particle, a missing solder joint. A single unit can carry many defects, so you count them. The c and u charts track defects.

Defective unit

Also called a nonconforming unit

A unit with at least one defect serious enough to fail it. Each unit is either defective or not, so a door with three scratches still counts once. The p and np charts track defective units.

Handbook 6.3.3

The handbook draws a finer line. A nonconforming unit fails a specification, while a defective unit fails to work as intended, and a unit can be one without the other. Most texts treat the two words as the same, and so does this module. See section 6.3.3.

When defects are rare, almost every defective chip holds exactly one defect, and the two counts nearly agree. As the rate rises, chips start collecting a second and third defect, which add to c and leave np unchanged. If defects land at random with an average of \lambda per chip, a chip stays clean with probability e^{-\lambda}, so the expected fraction defective is 1 - e^{-\lambda}. At \lambda = 0.3 that is 0.26, close to 0.3. At \lambda = 1.5 it is 0.78, and it can never pass 1.

  • 7
  • 7/200 = 0.035

A p chart tracks defective units as a fraction of the sample, here 7 of 200 boards, or 0.035. The 19 bad joints are defects, which belong on a c or u chart.

The c chart counts defects

Think of a wafer as thousands of places where a particle could land. Each place has a small chance of catching one, and the places act independently. Under those conditions the number of defects on a wafer follows the Poisson distribution. Its one parameter, c, is both the mean and the variance, so the standard deviation of a count is \sqrt{c}.

The Poisson model is the limit of the binomial when there are many chances for a defect and each is unlikely. The handbook's rule of thumb asks for at least 20 opportunities, each with a probability of 0.05 or less. With 200 opportunities at 0.025 each, the binomial probability of exactly 3 defects is 0.1400, and the Poisson value with c = 5 is 0.1404.

\begin{aligned} UCL &= \bar{c} + 3\sqrt{\bar{c}} \\ \text{Center line} &= \bar{c} \\ LCL &= \bar{c} - 3\sqrt{\bar{c}} \end{aligned}
\bar{c}
average number of defects per sample over the Phase I data
\sqrt{\bar{c}}
estimated standard deviation of a single count

The size of each sample, called the inspection unit, must stay the same: one wafer, one door, or a fixed group such as five wafers. If the formula gives a negative LCL, set it to zero. Counts cannot fall below zero, so the chart then has no working lower limit.

The handbook's example counts defects on 25 successive wafers, each holding 100 chips. The wafers carry 400 defects in total, so \bar{c} = 400/25 = 16 and \sqrt{16} = 4. The limits are 16 \pm 12, so UCL = 28 and LCL = 4.

Wafer 24, with 31 defects, lies above the UCL and signals. Wafer 3 has 28 defects and sits exactly on the UCL. A point has to fall beyond a limit to signal, so wafer 3 does not count, though an engineer would make a note of it.

Suppose the engineers trace wafer 24 to an assignable cause. Phase I practice from module 1 says to drop that point and recompute the limits from the rest. Tick the box to do it. The average falls, the UCL comes down with it, and wafer 3 now lies beyond the line. Removing one outlier often exposes the next, which is why Phase I repeats until no point with a known cause remains. A point with no cause found stays in.

With wafer 24 removed, the other 24 wafers hold 369 defects. What is the new UCL? Give two decimals.

\bar{c} = 369/24 = 15.375 and \sqrt{15.375} = 3.921, so UCL = 15.375 + 3 × 3.921 = 27.14. Wafer 3, with 28 defects, now lies above it. The LCL becomes 15.375 − 11.763 = 3.61.

The u chart allows unequal samples

The c chart assumes every sample is the same size. Often they are not. Rolls of coated film come off the line at different lengths, and boards arrive in lots of different sizes. Twelve defects on 60 m² of film is a better result than twelve on 20 m², so raw counts stop being comparable.

The fix is to divide each count by the amount inspected, measured in inspection units. If the inspection unit for film is 1 m², a 20 m² roll is 20 units. The u chart plots defects per unit.

u_i = \frac{c_i}{n_i}, \qquad \bar{u} = \frac{\sum c_i}{\sum n_i}, \qquad UCL_i,\ LCL_i = \bar{u} \pm 3\sqrt{\frac{\bar{u}}{n_i}}
c_i
defects found in sample i
n_i
size of sample i, in inspection units
\bar{u}
total defects divided by total inspection units over the Phase I samples

The limits follow from the Poisson model. A sample of n_i units has a defect count with mean and variance n_i u. Dividing the count by n_i divides its variance by n_i^2, which leaves u/n_i. A bigger sample averages over more film, so its limits sit closer to the center line. Every sample gets its own pair of limits, and the limit lines on the chart become steps.

Common mistake

Averaging the u_i to get \bar{u}. A plain average gives a 10 m² roll the same weight as a 100 m² roll. Pool instead, dividing total defects by total units. The same rule holds for \bar{p} on a p chart with unequal samples.

A u chart has \bar{u} = 2.0 defects per m². Roll A covers 4 m² and roll B covers 16 m². Compared with roll A, how far is roll B's UCL from the center line?

  • The same distance, because ū is the same
  • Half as far
  • A quarter as far
  • Twice as far

The distance is 3\sqrt{\bar{u}/n_i}. For roll A it is 3\sqrt{2/4} = 2.12, and for roll B it is 3\sqrt{2/16} = 1.06, exactly half. Quadrupling the area halves the width.

The p and np charts count defective units

When each unit is judged pass or fail, the natural model is the binomial distribution. The handbook lists its assumptions: the process is stable, every unit has the same probability p of being defective, and units are independent of one another. In a sample of n units, the number defective D then has mean np and variance np(1-p). The fraction defective \hat{p} = D/n has mean p and variance p(1-p)/n.

\bar{p} \pm 3\sqrt{\frac{\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})}{n}}, \qquad \bar{p} = \frac{\sum D_i}{\sum n_i}

The np chart plots the count D_i itself. It needs a constant sample size and has limits n\bar{p} \pm 3\sqrt{n\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})}. Multiply every number on a p chart by n and you have the np chart, with the same points inside and outside the limits. Operators often prefer it because they write down a count and skip the division.

The handbook's p chart example tracks misregistration on 30 wafers. On each wafer, 50 chips were measured and the fraction with a misregistration was recorded. The handbook shows the finished chart only as an image, so the numbers here are recomputed from its data. The 30 wafers had 347 misregistered chips out of 1,500, so \bar{p} = 347/1500 = 0.2313. The standard deviation of one wafer's fraction is \sqrt{0.2313 \times 0.7687 / 50} = 0.0596.

Using \bar{p} = 0.2313 and n = 50, what is the p chart's UCL? Give three decimals.

UCL = 0.2313 + 3 × 0.0596 = 0.410. The LCL is 0.2313 − 0.1789 = 0.052. In the figure below, wafers 15 (0.44) and 23 (0.48) lie above the UCL.

With a constant sample size the limits are flat lines. In the third view each wafer gets limits from its own n_i around the pooled \bar{p}, so the limits step in and out like those of a u chart. In the starting draw, wafer 15 shows 0.44 from only 27 chips and stays inside its wide limits. Wafer 21 shows 0.41 from 59 chips and signals. For the smallest samples the LCL formula goes negative, and the chart sets it to zero.

Negative lower limits are common on p charts. A p chart has a positive LCL only when n > 9(1-p)/p. At p = 0.01 that means more than 891 units per sample. With smaller samples the chart can flag a rise in defectives and never a fall, so it cannot confirm that an improvement worked.

When three-sigma limits mislead

Three-sigma limits borrow their promise from the normal curve. Each tail beyond a limit should hold 0.135% of in-control points, for a false alarm about once in 370 samples. A Poisson count is only roughly normal. It cannot go below zero, and for small means it piles up near zero with a long tail to the right. The handbook calls the normal approximation adequate once the mean reaches about 5.

The lower limit suffers first. Solving c - 3\sqrt{c} > 0 shows that the LCL is positive only when c is above 9. The handbook works the case c = 10. The LCL is 10 - 3\sqrt{10} = 0.513, so only a count of zero falls below it. The probability of zero defects is e^{-10} = 0.000045, about 1/30 of the 0.00135 the limit was meant to deliver. At the same time the upper tail holds 0.35%, 2.6 times its share.

The two errors pull in opposite directions. The chart raises false alarms on the high side more often than promised and almost never signals on the low side. A drop in defects is good news worth confirming, and this chart will rarely confirm it. For c = 10 the handbook suggests raising the lower limit to 2 or 3. The probability of 1 or fewer defects is 0.0005, and of 2 or fewer is 0.0028, both near the intended 0.00135.

A c chart has \bar{c} = 6 defects per panel. What does the three-sigma formula give for the lower limit, and what follows?

  • About −1.35, so the LCL is set to zero and the chart cannot signal a drop in defects
  • Exactly 0, so a panel with zero defects is a signal
  • 6 − 3 × 6 = −12, so the chart needs a new c̄
  • About 1.35, so a panel with 0 or 1 defects is a signal

\sqrt{6} = 2.449, so the formula gives 6 − 7.35 = −1.35. Counts cannot be negative, so the LCL is set to zero and nothing can fall beyond it. The UCL is 6 + 7.35 = 13.35. To detect an improvement at this defect rate, use one of the alternatives in the next section.

Better limits for small counts

The handbook offers two remedies. The first changes the data so that normal-theory limits fit. The second keeps the data and takes the limits from the Poisson distribution itself.

Variance-stabilizing transformations

The trouble with a Poisson count is that its spread grows with its mean. A square root undoes that. For a Poisson count c, the quantity 2\sqrt{c} has a variance close to 1 whatever the mean, and its distribution is closer to symmetric. The handbook lists three versions:

y = 2\sqrt{c}, \qquad y = 2\sqrt{c + 3/8}, \qquad y = \sqrt{c} + \sqrt{c+1}

The first is the plain square root. The second is due to Anscombe (1948) and the third to Freeman and Tukey (1950). Both refinements hold the variance nearer to 1 for small means. At a mean of 5, the variance of 2\sqrt{c} is 1.14, while the Anscombe and Freeman–Tukey versions both give 1.00. Since the transformed values have a standard deviation of about 1, the limits are simply \bar{y} \pm 3, where \bar{y} is the average of the transformed Phase I counts. The cost is the scale. Nobody on a production floor thinks in units of 2\sqrt{c}, so translate the limits back into counts when you label the chart.

Probability limits

The second remedy skips the normal curve. Choose the UCL as the smallest count whose upper-tail probability is at most 0.00135, and choose the LCL the same way from below. The chart stays in counts, and neither tail ever holds more than its share. The handbook points to tables by Molina (1973); today a spreadsheet or a few lines of code finds the limits. For the wafers with \bar{c} = 16, probability limits signal at 4 or fewer defects and at 30 or more. Wafer 24 still signals and wafer 3 still does not.

Counts are whole numbers, so no method hits 0.135% exactly. The sawtooth comes from the limits jumping from one whole count to the next as the mean grows. Three-sigma limits run hot in the upper tail, at 1.5 to 3.5 times the target once a lower limit exists, and cold in the lower tail, at under half the target. The transformations shrink that imbalance and reverse it. For means above 5, their upper tail typically holds about 0.6 times its share and their lower tail about 1.6 to 1.9 times, and they give a lower limit from a mean of about 3 to 4.5. Probability limits never exceed the target in either tail, at the price of usually falling short of it.

Why might an engineer choose probability limits over the Freeman–Tukey transformation for a c chart?

  • Probability limits put exactly 0.135% in each tail
  • The transformation only works when c̄ is above 9
  • Probability limits need no Phase I data
  • Probability limits keep the chart in the original units of defects

Both methods fix most of the imbalance between the tails. Probability limits keep the chart in counts, so an operator reads defects per wafer directly. A transformed chart needs its limits translated back before most people can use it.

Takeaways

  • A defect is one flaw. A defective unit has at least one defect and counts once. Chart defects with c or u charts and defective units with p or np charts.
  • The c chart rests on the Poisson model, with limits \bar{c} \pm 3\sqrt{\bar{c}}. The u chart divides each count by the sample size and gives each sample limits \bar{u} \pm 3\sqrt{\bar{u}/n_i}.
  • The p chart rests on the binomial model, with limits \bar{p} \pm 3\sqrt{\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})/n}. The np chart plots the count for a fixed n and gives the same signals.
  • Unequal sample sizes give stepped limits around a pooled center line. A negative LCL is set to zero, and the chart then cannot signal an improvement.
  • For small mean counts, three-sigma limits give too many false alarms above and too few below. Square-root transformations or probability limits restore the balance.
