EWMA charts

An EWMA chart plots a running average that keeps every past observation but trusts the newest most. One number, λ, sets how fast old data fade, and with it how sensitive the chart is to small shifts.

A weighted memory

A Shewhart chart bases each decision on the latest point alone. The exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) chart, introduced by S. W. Roberts in 1959, bases each decision on all the data so far and gives older observations less and less weight. The statistic is updated with one line of arithmetic:

\text{EWMA}_t = \lambda\, Y_t + (1-\lambda)\,\text{EWMA}_{t-1}, \qquad t = 1, 2, \ldots, n
Y_t
the observation at time t
\text{EWMA}_0
the starting value: the target, or the mean of in-control historical data
\lambda
the weighting constant, 0 < \lambda \le 1

Substitute the recursion into itself and each observation's weight appears. The newest gets \lambda, the one before \lambda(1-\lambda), the one before that \lambda(1-\lambda)^2, and so on:

\text{EWMA}_t = \lambda \sum_{i=0}^{t-1} (1-\lambda)^i\, Y_{t-i} + (1-\lambda)^t\, \text{EWMA}_0

The weights shrink geometrically, and together with the weight left on the starting value they add to 1. A large \lambda forgets quickly. At \lambda = 1 the EWMA equals the latest observation, and the chart becomes a Shewhart chart. A small \lambda remembers a long way back, which averages away noise and lets a small, lasting shift build up.

What does an EWMA chart with \lambda = 1 plot?

  • The average of every observation so far
  • The starting value EWMA₀ at every period
  • The latest observation, the same as a Shewhart chart
  • A 5-point moving average

With \lambda = 1 the recursion becomes \text{EWMA}_t = Y_t + 0 \cdot \text{EWMA}_{t-1} = Y_t. The chart plots each observation on its own, which is a Shewhart chart for individual values.

Control limits

Averaging shrinks variation, so the EWMA needs narrower limits than the raw data. If the observations have standard deviation s, estimated from in-control historical data, the variance of the EWMA settles down to

s^2_{\text{ewma}} = \frac{\lambda}{2-\lambda}\, s^2

and the control limits sit k of those standard deviations either side of the starting value:

\text{UCL} = \text{EWMA}_0 + k\, s_{\text{ewma}}
\text{LCL} = \text{EWMA}_0 - k\, s_{\text{ewma}}

The handbook writes k for the width of the limits, usually 3. It is a different quantity from the allowance k of a CUSUM. At \lambda = 0.3, \sqrt{\lambda/(2-\lambda)} = 0.42, so the EWMA's limits are 42% as wide as limits for single observations.

The variance formula holds once the chart has run for a while. In the first few periods the EWMA still leans on \text{EWMA}_0, a fixed number with no variance, so its true standard deviation is smaller:

s_{\text{ewma}}(t) = s\,\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{2-\lambda}\left[1-(1-\lambda)^{2t}\right]}

The bracket equals 1-(1-\lambda)^2 at t = 1 and climbs toward 1, so these exact limits start narrow and fan out to the asymptotic ones. With \lambda = 0.3 they are within 0.01 of the asymptotic limits by about period 7. Exact limits catch a process that starts off target a little sooner. The handbook's example uses the asymptotic limits.

The handbook's example uses \text{EWMA}_0 = 50, s = 2.0539, \lambda = 0.3 and k = 3. Compute the asymptotic UCL to two decimals.

\sqrt{0.3/1.7} = 0.4201, so s_{\text{ewma}} = 0.4201 \times 2.0539 = 0.8628. The UCL is 50 + 3 \times 0.8628 = 52.59 and the LCL is 47.41. The handbook prints 52.5884 and 47.4115.

The handbook example

The handbook's example monitors 20 observations of a process with a historical mean of 50 and standard deviation s = 2.0539, using \lambda = 0.3 and k = 3. The first EWMA is 0.3 \times 52.0 + 0.7 \times 50 = 50.60, and each later value follows from the one before.

The handbook concludes that the process is in control and draws attention to the upward drift over the last five periods. The raw values have all been above 50 since period 16. One more reading of 54.0 or higher in period 21 would carry the EWMA past the UCL, so this is a chart to keep watching and a good moment to ask what changed around period 16.

Common mistake

Do not judge raw observations against the EWMA's limits. Five observations here lie outside 47.41 to 52.59, and none of them is a signal. The limits belong to the EWMA statistic; single observations have their own, wider limits.

In the handbook example \text{EWMA}_1 = 50.60, the second observation is Y_2 = 47.0 and \lambda = 0.3. Compute \text{EWMA}_2 to two decimals.

\text{EWMA}_2 = 0.3 \times 47.0 + 0.7 \times 50.60 = 14.10 + 35.42 = 49.52, the handbook's second value. The new observation sits 3.6 below the previous EWMA, and the EWMA moves only 0.3 \times 3.6 = 1.08 toward it.

Choosing λ and k

The two settings work together. A small \lambda gives the chart a long memory, which suits small shifts, and it also narrows the limits, so k has to be chosen to keep false alarms rare. Hunter (1986) suggests a \lambda between 0.2 and 0.3, a choice the handbook calls somewhat arbitrary. Lucas and Saccucci (1990) published tables of run lengths for many pairs of \lambda and k, so you can pick the pair that keeps false alarms as rare as you need and detects your shift of interest fastest.

Each pair below comes from those tables and gives an in-control average run length (ARL) of about 500 observations. The run lengths after a shift were computed for this course with a Markov-chain approximation.

You need to catch shifts of about 0.5σ quickly, with an in-control ARL near 500. Based on the table, which design should you pick?

  • λ = 0.40 with k = 3.054
  • λ = 0.05 with k = 2.615
  • λ = 1, a Shewhart chart with 3.09σ limits
  • Any of them, since they share the same in-control ARL

For a 0.5σ shift, λ = 0.05 has the shortest ARL in the table, 28.8 observations. A long memory lets a small, persistent shift accumulate. The price is slower response to large shifts: at 3σ it needs 3.50 observations against 2.02 for λ = 0.4.

Using an EWMA chart well

The starting value \text{EWMA}_0 and the standard deviation s come from historical data, and the handbook stresses that those data must describe the process while it was in control. If they do not, finish the Phase I work described in module 1 first: find the special causes in the history, remove the affected data, and only then compute the baseline.

The limits also assume independent observations, and Lucas and Saccucci's tables assume normal data. Positively autocorrelated data, common in continuous and chemical processes, make the EWMA vary more than the formula expects, so false alarms become frequent. Module 11 covers models for such data.

Your historical data include a week when a heater failed and the process ran hot. What should you do before computing \text{EWMA}_0 and s?

  • Remove the heater week, since its cause is known, and compute the baseline from the remaining in-control data
  • Keep all the data, because the EWMA smooths out unusual periods
  • Use a small λ so the hot week carries little weight

The baseline must describe the process when only common causes act. A period with a known special cause belongs outside it. Left in, it would shift \text{EWMA}_0, inflate s, and make the chart slower to notice the next real problem.

The EWMA as a forecast

The EWMA through period t is also a sensible forecast of the observation at period t+1: a weighted average of the past that leans on recent data. Forecasters call the same recursion simple exponential smoothing, and they choose \lambda to make the one-step forecast errors small.

Tuned for the same shift and the same in-control ARL, EWMA and CUSUM charts detect small shifts about equally fast. A two-sided CUSUM with k = 0.5\sigma and h = 5\sigma has an in-control ARL of 465 and catches a 1σ shift in 10.4 samples on average; an EWMA with \lambda = 0.1 and k = 2.814 manages 500 and 10.3. The EWMA's plotted value is an average on the scale of the data, which makes it easy to read alongside the measurements.

Takeaways

  • The EWMA updates as \lambda Y_t + (1-\lambda)\,\text{EWMA}_{t-1}. Weights fall off geometrically with age, and \lambda = 1 gives a Shewhart chart.
  • The limits are \text{EWMA}_0 \pm k\, s\sqrt{\lambda/(2-\lambda)}. Exact limits include the factor 1-(1-\lambda)^{2t} and start narrower.
  • Small \lambda (0.05 to 0.2) suits small shifts; larger \lambda reacts faster to large ones. Hunter suggests 0.2 to 0.3, and Lucas and Saccucci's tables pair each \lambda with a k.
  • In the handbook example every EWMA value stays inside 47.41 to 52.59, but the last five periods drift upward and deserve attention.
  • Base \text{EWMA}_0 and s on in-control historical data, and check that observations are roughly independent.
