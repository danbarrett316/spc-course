Variation and control

No two bottles from a filling line hold exactly the same amount. Statistical process control starts by separating the variation that is built into a process from the variation that tells you something has changed.

Every process varies

Measure anything a process makes closely enough and the numbers disagree. Fill volumes, shaft diameters, invoice processing times, the width of a line etched on a silicon wafer: each one scatters around a typical value. That scatter is not a malfunction. It is what the process does.

The useful question is narrower: is the scatter stable? A stable process is like a fair die. You cannot predict the next roll, but you can say with confidence what the next hundred rolls will look like. An unstable process is a die that someone keeps swapping out while you are not looking.

The histogram answers what values do we get? The time-ordered plot answers are we still getting the same thing? Every control chart in this course is built on the second view. Time order is the evidence.

Common causes and special causes

Walter Shewhart, a physicist at Bell Telephone Laboratories, gave this idea its working vocabulary in the 1920s. He split variation into two kinds that call for completely different responses.

Common causes

Shewhart called them chance causes

Many small influences that are always present: room temperature, small differences between lots of raw material, operator technique, measurement noise. Each contributes a little, and together they produce steady, random-looking scatter. They belong to the system, so only a change to the system reduces them.

Special causes

Shewhart called them assignable causes

Something specific that is not part of the usual process: a worn tool, a new supplier lot, a mis-set dial, a power dip. Special causes show up as shifts, trends, spikes and other patterns. The people running the process can usually find and remove them.

Key idea

A process whose variation comes only from common causes is in statistical control. Its future output is predictable within limits. SPC is the discipline of catching special causes quickly without reacting to common-cause noise.

A filling line's volumes vary by about ±3 mL from bottle to bottle because of small fluctuations in line pressure, foam and valve timing. Nothing about the line has changed in months. This variation is best described as:

  • Special-cause variation
  • Common-cause variation
  • Measurement error only

Many small, ever-present influences that together produce steady scatter are common causes. Reducing them means changing the system: better valves, tighter pressure regulation, a different nozzle design.

On Tuesday morning a new lot of caps arrives, and for two hours the fills run about 2 mL heavier than usual. What kind of cause is this?

  • Common cause, because fills always vary
  • Special cause, because something specific changed
  • It cannot be either until the process is out of specification

A shift that starts with an identifiable event (a new lot) is a special, or assignable, cause. Whether or not the heavier bottles are still within specification, the process has changed and someone should find out why.

The cost of reacting to noise

If common-cause noise is part of the system, what happens when someone treats it like a signal? Suppose an operator weighs every bottle and turns the fill dial by exactly the amount the last bottle missed target. It sounds diligent. W. Edwards Deming called it tampering, and it makes the process worse.

The reason is simple. The last bottle's error was mostly random, so it says nothing about the next bottle. Correcting for it adds a second random disturbance on top of the first. If the noise has standard deviation \sigma, the adjusted process has standard deviation \sqrt{2}\,\sigma, about 41% more spread.

The third rule is a control chart in miniature: leave the process alone while it behaves like itself, and act when the data give strong evidence that something changed. The rest of this course is about building that rule well.

A stable filling line has a bottle-to-bottle standard deviation of 1.0 mL. An operator starts adjusting the dial after every bottle by the amount it missed target. Roughly what standard deviation should you now expect?

  • About 0.5 mL, half as much
  • About 1.0 mL, unchanged
  • About 1.4 mL
  • About 2.0 mL

Each bottle now carries its own noise plus the reversed noise of the previous bottle. Two independent disturbances of variance \sigma^2 add to 2\sigma^2, so the standard deviation becomes \sqrt{2}\times 1.0 \approx 1.41 mL.

Where SPC came from

Quality control is older than statistics. Medieval guilds controlled quality with long apprenticeships and proof of skill. What changed in the twentieth century was mass production: telephone equipment, for instance, needed millions of interchangeable parts, and nobody could inspect quality into every one of them.

On 16 May 1924, Shewhart sent his manager at Bell Labs a one-page memo with a sketch of what we now call a control chart. His 1931 book, Economic Control of Quality of Manufactured Product, set out the theory. His colleagues Harold Dodge and Harry Romig applied the same statistics to sampling inspection. Together their work is the foundation of the methods in this course.

Monitoring, then control

The handbook draws a line between two families of tools. Statistical process control watches the process while it runs, using control charts to compare what is happening now with what happened before. Statistical quality control inspects finished product, using sampling plans to decide whether a lot is fit to ship. Most of this course is about the first; module 9 covers the second.

Process control itself happens in two phases.

  1. Phase I: establish the baseline. Collect historical data, compute trial control limits, and investigate any points outside them. If a point has a known assignable cause, remove it and recompute. Repeat until the remaining data describe the process when it is running as it should.
  2. Phase II: monitor. Freeze the Phase I limits and plot new data against them as they arrive. A signal means today's process no longer looks like the baseline.

When a Phase II chart signals, someone has to act. The handbook names two ways to do it. An out-of-control action plan (OCAP) is a flowchart, written in advance for each chart, that tells the engineer what to check and in what order. An advanced process control loop automates the response, adjusting the process by an amount proportional to the deviation.

In control is not the same as good

Control limits and specification limits answer different questions, and mixing them up is one of the most common mistakes in quality work.

Control limits

The voice of the process

Calculated from the process's own data. They describe what the process does when only common causes act. They say nothing about what the customer needs.

Specification limits

The voice of the customer

Set by the design or the customer: this bottle must hold between 496 and 504 mL. They say nothing about what the process is capable of.

Put the two together and a process can be in one of four states. The terms below come from Donald Wheeler, and the actions follow the handbook's sections on processes that are out of control and processes that are in control but unacceptable.

Two lessons follow. First, a process must be stable before you can judge its capability, because an unstable process has no single capability to measure. Second, when a stable process makes bad product, hunting for special causes is pointless. The bad product is coming from common causes, and the fix is a change to the process: a designed experiment, recalibration, a better machine, or a fresh look at historical data. Module 8 shows how to measure the gap.

A stable process puts 2% of its bottles below the 496 mL specification. No points fall outside its control limits and the run rules are quiet. Which response fits?

  • Investigate each low bottle to find its special cause
  • Widen the control limits until the low bottles fit
  • Change the process: reduce its spread or move its average
  • Nothing, because the process is in control

This is the threshold state. The process is predictable, and it predictably makes some nonconforming product. Only a change to the system, such as recentering the fill or reducing variation, will fix it.

Which statement about control limits and specification limits is true?

  • Control limits come from the customer; specification limits come from process data
  • Control limits are calculated from process data; specification limits are set by the design or customer
  • They should always be equal for a well-run process

Control limits are the voice of the process; specification limits are the voice of the customer. Capability analysis, in module 8, compares the two.

Takeaways

  • All processes vary. The question is whether the variation is stable over time.
  • Common causes are many, small and always present. Special causes are specific, identifiable and intermittent.
  • Adjusting a stable process in response to noise (tampering) increases variation.
  • SPC monitors the process in two phases: set limits from a clean baseline, then watch new data against them.
  • Control limits are the voice of the process. Specification limits are the voice of the customer. A process can be in control and still unacceptable.
