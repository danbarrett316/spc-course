CUSUM charts

A Shewhart chart judges each sample on its own, so a small, lasting shift in the mean can hide for dozens of samples. A cumulative sum chart adds up every deviation from target, and a persistent shift soon piles up into a clear signal.

Why Shewhart charts are slow on small shifts

An \bar{X} chart asks one question of each new subgroup: does this average fall outside \pm 3\sigma_{\bar{x}}? That rule catches large, sudden changes almost at once. It is poor at catching small ones, because it forgets everything it saw before the latest point.

Suppose the mean moves up by one standard error, 1\sigma_{\bar{x}}. The upper limit is now only 2\sigma_{\bar{x}} above the new mean, so each subgroup has about a 2.3% chance of crossing it. You wait about 44 subgroups for a signal on average, and a shift of 0.5\sigma_{\bar{x}} takes about 155. The handbook's run-length analysis finds that a cumulative sum (CUSUM) chart detects shifts of about 2\sigma_{\bar{x}} or less faster than a Shewhart chart does.

The comparison is fair. With h = 5\sigma_{\bar{x}} this CUSUM raises a false alarm about once every 465 samples, less often than the \bar{X} chart's once every 370. It is the more cautious chart and still the faster one for small shifts.

An \bar{X} chart has 3σ limits. The process mean moves up by exactly 1\sigma_{\bar{x}} and stays there. About how many subgroups pass, on average, before one falls above the UCL?

  • About 2
  • About 370
  • About 44
  • Never, because the shift is smaller than the limits

After the shift the UCL is 2\sigma_{\bar{x}} above the new mean. The chance that a normal value lands more than 2 standard deviations above its mean is 0.0228, so the average wait is 1/0.0228 \approx 44 subgroups.

Add up the deviations

The fix is memory. Instead of plotting each subgroup average, plot the running total of its deviations from the in-control mean:

S_m = \sum_{i=1}^{m} \left(\bar{x}_i - \hat{\mu}_0\right)
\bar{x}_i
the average of subgroup i
\hat{\mu}_0
the in-control mean, estimated from Phase I data
m
the number of the latest subgroup

While the process stays on target, the deviations are positive about as often as negative, and the sum wanders around zero with no trend. When the mean moves to \hat{\mu}_0 + \delta, each new subgroup adds \delta on average, so the sum climbs along a line of slope \delta. A shift too small to push any single point past a control limit still bends the line. Some software divides each deviation by \sigma_{\bar{x}} so the sum is in standard-error units; the shape of the plot stays the same.

Key idea

On a cumulative sum chart the height of the line means little on its own. The slope carries the information: a flat stretch says the mean is on target, and a steady slope of \delta says the mean has moved by \delta.

In the handbook example the cumulative sum is −2.50 after group 12 and 19.08 after group 20 (target 325). Estimate the process mean over groups 13 to 20, to two decimals.

The sum rose by 19.08 - (-2.50) = 21.58 over 8 groups, a slope of 21.58 / 8 = 2.70. Each group added 2.70 on average, so the mean over that stretch was about 325 + 2.70 = 327.70.

The V-mask

A cumulative sum makes a shift visible, but someone still has to decide when a slope is steep enough to act on. In 1959 G. A. Barnard proposed a V-shaped template for that job, the V-mask. The handbook describes its use in four steps.

  1. Place the mask. Put the mask's origin on the latest point of the cumulative sum, with the V lying on its side and opening back toward earlier samples.
  2. Read its shape. The vertex sits a lead distance d ahead of the origin. The arms slope back from the vertex at \pm k per sample, so at the origin they are h = dk above and below the point.
  3. Look for points outside the arms. If any earlier point lies outside, the process is out of control. A point below the lower arm means the sum has since risen faster than the arm allows, so the mean has moved up. A point above the upper arm means it has moved down.
  4. Slide the mask back. Move the origin to earlier points to find the first one that signals. That tells you roughly when the shift began.

Designing the mask

The handbook gives a design recipe built on two error rates: \alpha, the probability of a false alarm, and \beta, the probability of missing a shift of \delta standard errors.

k = \frac{\delta\,\sigma_{\bar{x}}}{2}
d = \frac{2}{\delta^2}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha}\right)
h = d\,k

For the example, the handbook takes \alpha = 0.0027 (the false alarm rate of a 3σ Shewhart chart), \beta = 0.01 and \delta = 1. The process standard deviation is 1.27 and n = 4, so \sigma_{\bar{x}} = 1.27/\sqrt{4} = 0.635 and k = 0.3175. The formula for d then gives d = 11.81 and h = 3.75. The handbook's worked table uses a slightly wider h = 4.1959, which corresponds to d \approx 13.2. Both values first signal at group 14.

Many practitioners skip \alpha and \beta. A rule of thumb from Montgomery, which the handbook quotes, sets k to half the shift you want to catch and h to about 4 or 5, both measured in units of \sigma_{\bar{x}}. To catch a 1\sigma_{\bar{x}} shift, use k = 0.5\sigma_{\bar{x}} and h = 4\sigma_{\bar{x}} or 5\sigma_{\bar{x}}.

With the V-mask on the latest point, an earlier point falls below the lower arm. What does that indicate?

  • The process mean has shifted up
  • The process mean has shifted down
  • The process variation has increased
  • Nothing, because only points beyond the vertex count

The lower arm marks how far the sum may fall below the latest point while the mean is on target. A point beneath it means the sum has since risen by more than h plus k for every sample in between, which only a sustained upward shift produces.

The tabular CUSUM

The tabular form does the V-mask's work with two running sums and no geometry. One sum collects evidence of an upward shift, the other evidence of a downward shift:

S_{\text{hi}}(i) = \max\!\left(0,\; S_{\text{hi}}(i-1) + \bar{x}_i - \hat{\mu}_0 - k\right)
S_{\text{lo}}(i) = \max\!\left(0,\; S_{\text{lo}}(i-1) + \hat{\mu}_0 - k - \bar{x}_i\right)

Both sums start at zero, and the process is out of control when either one exceeds the decision interval h. The allowance k is subtracted every time, so deviations smaller than k drain the sum and ordinary noise keeps it near zero. The floor at zero stops the sum from banking credit while the process runs on the other side of target, so it reacts as soon as a shift arrives. You can work in data units, as the handbook's example does, or divide everything by \sigma_{\bar{x}}.

The two methods are the same test. S_{\text{hi}}(i) equals the largest total of \bar{x} - \hat{\mu}_0 - k over any run of recent samples ending at sample i, or zero if every such total is negative. A point falls below the V-mask's lower arm exactly when one of those totals exceeds h.

The handbook's h = 4.1959 is 6.6\sigma_{\bar{x}}, well above the rule of thumb. That buys a very long in-control run, about 4,700 samples between false alarms on the upper sum, at the price of slower detection. A 1\sigma_{\bar{x}} shift takes about 13.6 samples to detect on average, against 8.4 with h = 4\sigma_{\bar{x}}.

The shift in this example is also large. Groups 13 to 20 average 327.70, about 4.2\sigma_{\bar{x}} above target, so an \bar{X} chart with the same \sigma_{\bar{x}} would flag group 13 by itself, one group before the CUSUM. The example shows the mechanics clearly. Figure 6.1 shows the shifts where a CUSUM earns its place.

Common mistake

After a signal, once the cause is found and fixed, reset both sums to zero. Left alone, a large upper sum drains by only about k per sample, so in this example a sum of 19 would keep signalling for dozens of samples after the process was back on target.

In the handbook example \hat{\mu}_0 = 325, k = 0.3175 and the upper sum after group 13 is 3.0075. Group 14 has \bar{x} = 327.25. Compute S_{\text{hi}}(14) to two decimals.

S_{\text{hi}}(14) = \max(0,\; 3.0075 + 327.25 - 325 - 0.3175) = 4.94. That exceeds h = 4.1959, so group 14 is the first out-of-control signal, as in the handbook's table.

Choosing k and h with average run length

The average run length (ARL) is the average number of subgroups a chart plots before it signals. A good design has a long ARL while the process is in control, so false alarms are rare, and a short ARL once the mean has shifted. The two parameters split the work:

  • k sets which shift the chart is tuned for. Half the shift you care about is the usual choice.
  • h trades false alarms against speed. A larger h lengthens every run length, in control and out.

The handbook compares a CUSUM with k = 0.5\sigma_{\bar{x}} against a 3σ \bar{X} chart. The table repeats that comparison with values recomputed for this course.

The CUSUM columns are for a single sum watching for an upward shift, which is how the handbook sets up its comparison with the two-sided \bar{X} chart. Running both sums together roughly halves the in-control ARL, to about 168 for h = 4 and 465 for h = 5. The values come from a Markov-chain approximation (Brook and Evans) and agree with the handbook's table to within one unit in the last digit, with one exception: for a 0.5\sigma_{\bar{x}} shift and h = 5 the handbook prints 30.0, where the calculation gives 38.0.

Above the break-even point the Shewhart chart wins. A single extreme point is all it needs, while the CUSUM must first subtract its allowance and build up past h. That is why the two are often run side by side, with the CUSUM watching for small, persistent shifts and Shewhart limits catching large, sudden ones.

Takeaways

  • A Shewhart chart uses only the latest point, so it is slow to catch shifts of about 2\sigma_{\bar{x}} or less. A 1σ shift takes about 44 subgroups on average.
  • A CUSUM plots the running total of deviations from target. Its slope estimates the current mean, and a persistent shift bends it upward or downward.
  • The V-mask and the tabular CUSUM are the same test. The tabular form, with upper and lower sums that reset at zero, is the one to use in software.
  • Set the allowance k to half the shift you want to detect and the decision interval h to about 4 or 5 standard errors. Check the choice with average run lengths.
  • CUSUM charts win on small shifts and Shewhart charts on large ones, so the two work well side by side.
