Process capability

A process can be perfectly stable and still make parts the customer cannot use. Capability indices compare the natural spread of a stable process with the width of its specification, and turn that comparison into a number you can track and negotiate over.

Capability compares two widths

Module 1 separated two voices. Control limits are the voice of the process and come from its data. Specification limits are the voice of the customer and come from the drawing or the contract. A control chart tells you whether the process is stable. Capability analysis asks the next question. Given that it is stable, does it fit inside the specification?

For a stable process whose output is normally distributed, 99.73% of all values fall within \mu \pm 3\sigma. That band is 6\sigma wide, and it is called the natural tolerance of the process. The specification is \text{USL} - \text{LSL} wide. The simplest capability index is the ratio of the two widths.

C_p = \frac{\text{USL} - \text{LSL}}{6\sigma}
\text{USL}, \text{LSL}
upper and lower specification limits
\sigma
standard deviation of the process, estimated by s from data collected while the process was in control

When C_p = 1, the natural spread exactly fills the specification. When C_p = 2, the process uses half of it. Below 1, some product falls outside the limits even when nothing goes wrong.

Key idea

Capability only has a meaning for a process in statistical control. An unstable process has no single mean or standard deviation, so any index computed from its data describes a mixture of past states and predicts nothing about the next lot. Chart first, then measure capability.

An engineer collects 120 shaft diameters over a week to report C_{pk}. The X̄ chart for the same week shows a clear shift on Wednesday afternoon. What should she do?

  • Report the index anyway, since 120 values is a large sample
  • Delete the diameters that fall outside the specification and recompute
  • Find and remove the cause of the shift, then estimate capability from data collected while the process is stable
  • Collect another week of data so the shift averages out

Capability is a property of a stable process. The shift is a special cause, so the first job is to find and remove it. Once the chart shows control, data from that period describe one process, and its capability can be estimated.

Off-center processes and Cpk

C_p only compares widths. It says nothing about where the process sits. A process with C_p = 2 centered on the USL would put half its output above the limit. To account for location, measure the distance from the mean to each limit in units of 3\sigma, and keep the smaller one.

\begin{aligned} C_{pu} &= \frac{\text{USL} - \mu}{3\sigma} \qquad C_{pl} = \frac{\mu - \text{LSL}}{3\sigma} \\[4pt] C_{pk} &= \min\left(C_{pu},\, C_{pl}\right) \end{aligned}

When the process is centered, C_{pu} = C_{pl} = C_p. As it moves off center, C_{pk} falls in proportion to the distance. The handbook writes this with the scaled distance k between the mean and the midpoint m of the specification.

k = \frac{|m - \mu|}{(\text{USL} - \text{LSL})/2}, \qquad C_{pk} = C_p\,(1 - k)

Because 0 \le k \le 1 while the mean is inside the limits, C_{pk} can never exceed C_p. If a characteristic has only one limit, such as a maximum impurity, only C_{pu} or C_{pl} applies.

The handbook's worked example has LSL = 8, USL = 20, a sample standard deviation s = 2 and a sample mean \bar{x} = 16. Compute \hat{C}_{pk} to three decimals.

\hat{C}_p = 12/(6 \times 2) = 1.0. The midpoint is 14, so k = |14 - 16|/6 = 0.333 and \hat{C}_{pk} = 1.0 \times (1 - 0.333) = 0.667. Directly, \hat{C}_{pu} = (20 - 16)/6 = 0.667 and \hat{C}_{pl} = (16 - 8)/6 = 1.333, and the smaller one is 0.667.

Handbook 6.1.6

In the worked example, C_p = 1.0 says the process would just fit if it were centered at 14. With the mean at 16, C_{pk} = 0.667 falls short of 1.0, and the handbook concludes that the process should be recentered and its variation reduced.

Neither C_p nor C_{pk} knows about the target value T. A process can sit near one limit, far from target, and still have a respectable C_{pk} if its spread is small. The index C_{pm} charges for distance from target by adding it to the variance.

C_{pm} = \frac{\text{USL} - \text{LSL}}{6\sqrt{\sigma^2 + (\mu - T)^2}}

When the process is on target, C_{pm} = C_p. In the handbook example, with T = 14, C_{pm} = 12 / (6\sqrt{4 + 4}) = 0.707.

From an index to a defect rate

Under the normal model, each index translates into a fraction of product outside the specification. For a centered process the fraction is 2\,\Phi(-3C_p), where \Phi is the standard normal cumulative distribution. The handbook tabulates four common values.

Many customers, for example in automotive supply chains, ask for C_{pk} \ge 1.33 on an established process, which leaves a full \sigma of room between the natural spread and each limit.

An off-center process needs each side separately. The fraction above the USL is \Phi(-3C_{pu}) and the fraction below the LSL is \Phi(-3C_{pl}). In the handbook example, C_{pu} = 0.667 puts \Phi(-2) = 2.28\% above 20, while C_{pl} = 1.333 puts only 32 ppm below 8.

Common mistake

Quoting parts per million from a capability index assumes the normal curve holds far out in the tails, where you have the least data. A modest skew that is invisible in a histogram can change the tail fraction tenfold. Section 8.6 shows how much.

A stable, centered process has C_p = C_{pk} = 1.33 and normally distributed output. About what fraction of its output falls outside the specification?

  • 0.27%
  • About 0.6 ppm
  • None at all
  • About 63 ppm

C_p = 1.33 puts each limit 3 \times 1.33 = 4 standard deviations from the mean. The two tails beyond \pm 4\sigma hold 2\,\Phi(-4) = 0.0000633, about 63 ppm. The handbook rounds this to 64 ppm.

Estimates come with uncertainty

In practice you never know \mu and \sigma. You plug in \bar{x} and s and get estimates, written \hat{C}_p and \hat{C}_{pk}. Both move from sample to sample, and the movement is larger than most people expect. The handbook asks for about 50 independent values before treating an estimate as valid at all, and recommends at least 100 for a capability study.

For C_p, the chi-square distribution of s^2 gives an exact confidence interval. With \nu = n - 1 degrees of freedom and confidence 1 - \alpha:

\hat{C}_p\sqrt{\frac{\chi^2_{\alpha/2,\,\nu}}{\nu}} \;\le\; C_p \;\le\; \hat{C}_p\sqrt{\frac{\chi^2_{1-\alpha/2,\,\nu}}{\nu}}

For C_{pk} no exact interval exists. The handbook gives a normal approximation, intended for n \ge 25:

\hat{C}_{pk} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{9n} + \frac{\hat{C}_{pk}^2}{2(n-1)}}

With 100 measurements and an estimate of 1.33, the 95% interval for C_p runs from 1.14 to 1.51, and for C_{pk} from 1.13 to 1.53. A study that size can separate 1.33 from 1.0. It cannot separate 1.33 from 1.2.

A supplier sends \hat{C}_{pk} = 1.40 from a study of 30 parts. Your purchase agreement requires C_{pk} \ge 1.33. What can you conclude?

  • The requirement is met, because 1.40 is above 1.33
  • The approximate 95% interval runs from about 1.02 to 1.78, so the data cannot show the requirement is met
  • Nothing, because Cpk cannot be estimated from fewer than 100 parts

The half-width is 1.96\sqrt{1/270 + 1.96/58} = 0.38, so the interval is about 1.02 to 1.78. Values below 1.33 are entirely plausible. Ask for a larger study, or for the lower confidence bound, before accepting the claim.

Checking for normality

Every formula so far assumes the output follows a normal distribution, and the defect rates lean on that assumption hardest. The normal curve is symmetric and bell-shaped, and its mean and standard deviation fix it completely. About 68.27% of values fall within \pm 1\sigma of the mean, 95.45% within \pm 2\sigma and 99.73% within \pm 3\sigma. No real process is exactly normal. The practical question is whether the data are close enough in the tails, where the specification limits sit.

A histogram of 100 values is too lumpy to answer that. The standard tool is the normal probability plot. Sort the data, and plot each value against the z-score you would expect for a value of that rank in a normal sample. If the data are normal, the points follow a straight line whose intercept estimates the mean and whose slope estimates the standard deviation. Departures show up as shapes.

  • A curve that bends upward at the right end means a long right tail (right skew). Bending downward at the left end means a long left tail.
  • An S-shape that steepens at both ends means heavier tails than normal. One that flattens at both ends means shorter tails.
  • A flat step or gap in the middle points to two populations mixed together, such as two machines or two shifts.

On a normal probability plot of 150 coating thicknesses, the middle points follow the line closely, but the largest ten values bend sharply upward above it. What does that suggest?

  • A long right tail, so normal theory will understate how much product exceeds the USL
  • Heavier tails than normal on both sides
  • The data are normal, since most points follow the line

Values that sit above the line at the right end are larger than a normal distribution would produce for those ranks. That is right skew. Normal-theory C_{pu} will look better than the process really is, which is the problem the next section addresses.

When the data are not normal

Some characteristics are skewed by nature. Thickness, flatness, impurity levels and cycle times have a hard floor and a long upper tail. When the probability plot bends, the handbook offers two remedies.

Transform the data. The Box-Cox family of power transformations, defined for positive values, can often make skewed data close to normal.

x^{(\lambda)} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{x^{\lambda} - 1}{\lambda} & \lambda \ne 0 \\[6pt] \ln x & \lambda = 0 \end{cases}

The value \lambda = 1 leaves the shape unchanged, \lambda = 0.5 is close to a square root, \lambda = 0 is the log, and negative values pull in a long right tail harder still. Software picks the \lambda that maximizes the normal likelihood of the transformed data. You then transform the specification limits with the same \lambda, check that the probability plot is now straight, and compute the usual indices on the new scale. In the handbook's example, 42 microwave radiation readings give \hat\lambda \approx 0.3. If the data contain zeros or negative values, add a constant first.

Use percentiles instead of \sigma. For a normal distribution, the 0.135th and 99.865th percentiles sit exactly 3\sigma below and above the mean. The nonparametric indices replace \pm 3\sigma with those percentiles, written p(0.00135) and p(0.99865), and replace the mean with the median.

\begin{aligned} C_{np} &= \frac{\text{USL} - \text{LSL}}{p(0.99865) - p(0.00135)} \\[6pt] C_{npk} &= \frac{\min\left(\text{USL} - \text{median},\ \text{median} - \text{LSL}\right)}{\left[p(0.99865) - p(0.00135)\right]/2} \end{aligned}

For normal data these equal C_p and C_{pk}. For skewed data they use the real width of the distribution. The price is data. Estimating a point that only 0.135% of values exceed needs hundreds or thousands of measurements.

Two lessons come out of the figure. First, the percentile index averages the two half-widths, so a long tail on one side is partly cancelled by a short tail on the other, and its far percentiles rest on the handful of largest and smallest values even in a sample of 1,000. Second, Box-Cox works well when some power of the data really is close to normal, which you can check on the transformed probability plot. When the methods disagree badly with each other or with normal theory, treat the defect rate as uncertain and measure it directly.

A stable process has a clearly skewed probability plot. Its normal-theory \hat{C}_{pk} is 1.35. What is the best next step?

  • Report 1.35, since the process is in control
  • Look for special causes until the data become normal
  • Transform the data with Box-Cox, or compute percentile-based indices, and compare the results with 1.35
  • Drop the most extreme values and recompute

The handbook's two remedies are a normalizing transformation, applied to the data and the limits, and indices built from the 0.135th and 99.865th percentiles. Either one uses the real shape of the distribution. If they come out well below 1.35, the normal-theory figure was flattering the process.

Takeaways

  • Capability compares the natural spread of a stable process, 6\sigma, with the specification width. It is meaningless for a process that is out of control.
  • C_p measures potential if the process were centered. C_{pk} = \min(C_{pu}, C_{pl}) = C_p(1-k) accounts for where the mean actually is. C_{pm} also charges for distance from target.
  • For a centered normal process, C_p of 1.00, 1.33, 1.67 and 2.00 correspond to about 0.27%, 63 ppm, 0.6 ppm and 2 ppb outside the limits.
  • Estimated indices vary a lot. Use at least 100 values when you can, and report a confidence interval with the estimate.
  • Check normality with a probability plot. For skewed data, use a Box-Cox transformation or percentile-based indices such as C_{npk}, and distrust tail rates from normal theory.
