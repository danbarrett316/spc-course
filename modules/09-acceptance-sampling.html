Lot acceptance sampling

A shipment of 10,000 parts arrives at the dock. Inspecting every one may be too slow, too expensive or, when the test destroys the part, impossible. Acceptance sampling inspects a random sample and uses it to accept or reject the whole lot, with risks you can choose in advance and calculate exactly.

What acceptance sampling is for

A lot is a batch of items made under the same conditions, such as a pallet of castings, a reel of resistors or a day's production of tablets. A sampling plan draws a random sample from the lot, inspects it, and applies a rule that decides the fate of the entire lot. The handbook places this between two extremes. With no inspection, defective product goes straight to the customer. With 100% inspection, every item is checked, which costs the most and is impossible when testing destroys the item. Sampling makes sense when the test is destructive, when full inspection is too costly, or when it takes too long.

Harold Dodge and Harry Romig built the theory and tables at Bell Telephone Laboratories, and the US military made acceptance sampling standard practice for munitions during the Second World War. Most plans work by attributes. Each sampled item either conforms or does not, and the rule counts the defectives. Plans by variables measure a characteristic instead and work with the sample mean and standard deviation. This module covers attribute plans, as the handbook does.

Common mistake

Acceptance sampling sorts lots. It does nothing to the process that made them. The lot rejected today came from the same process that will make tomorrow's lots. Dodge described single sampling plans as short-run, one-shot tests; long-run quality comes from process control, the subject of modules 2 to 7. Use sampling to protect the customer while process control does the improving, and inspect less as evidence of good quality builds up.

Your sampling plan rejects about 30% of the lots from one supplier. Rejected lots go back for sorting. What effect does the plan have on the supplier's process?

  • It gradually improves the process, because bad lots are removed
  • It guarantees that accepted lots contain no defectives
  • None. It keeps more bad lots away from you, but the process stays the same until someone changes it

Sampling separates lots into accepted and rejected. The rejection rate is a signal that the supplier's process needs attention, through process control, root-cause work or a better process. The plan itself changes nothing upstream.

One lot, one sample

The simplest plan is a single sampling plan, written (n, c). Draw n items at random and count the defectives d. Accept the lot if d \le c and reject it if d > c. The number c is the acceptance number. The handbook's running example is n = 52, c = 3.

The outcome for any one lot is partly luck. A sample of 52 from a lot that is 5% defective contains 2.6 defectives on average, but any given sample may hold 0, 3 or 6. When the lot is large compared with the sample, d follows a binomial distribution, and the probability that the plan accepts a lot with fraction defective p is

P_a = P(d \le c) = \sum_{d=0}^{c} \binom{n}{d}\, p^{d}\, (1-p)^{\,n-d}

The operating characteristic curve

Plot P_a against the lot fraction defective p and you get the plan's operating characteristic (OC) curve, the handbook's main tool for judging a plan. A perfect plan would accept every good lot and reject every bad one. A real plan makes two kinds of mistake, and each side of the transaction worries about one of them.

AQL and producer's risk

The producer's point, (AQL, 1 − α)

The acceptable quality level is the fraction defective that counts as normal, good output. The producer wants lots at the AQL accepted nearly every time. The probability of rejecting one anyway is the producer's risk, \alpha, a type I error.

LTPD and consumer's risk

The consumer's point, (LTPD, β)

The lot tolerance percent defective is a fraction defective the consumer will not tolerate. The consumer wants such lots rejected nearly every time. The probability of accepting one anyway is the consumer's risk, \beta, a type II error.

The handbook gives 0.01 to 0.2 as typical values for both risks. Together the two points pin down what a plan must do, and the OC curve shows whether it does.

For the handbook plan n = 52, c = 3, what is the probability of accepting a lot that is 5% defective? Give three decimals. Use the figure, or add up the four binomial terms.

P_a = \sum_{d=0}^{3}\binom{52}{d}(0.05)^d(0.95)^{52-d} = 0.0694 + 0.1901 + 0.2551 + 0.2237 = 0.738. The handbook's table prints 0.739, and 0.845 at 4% where the exact sum rounds to 0.846; every other entry agrees to three decimals. A lot with one item in twenty defective passes this plan about three times in four.

You double the sample size of the handbook plan to n = 104 but keep c = 3. What happens to the two risks at an AQL of 2% and an LTPD of 10%?

  • Both risks fall
  • The consumer's risk falls, and the producer's risk rises
  • The producer's risk falls, and the consumer's risk rises
  • Neither changes, because c is the same

Allowing the same three defectives in twice as many items is a stricter standard, so the whole curve moves left. At 10% defective, P_a drops from 0.223 to 0.006. At 2% defective, it drops from 0.980 to 0.844, so the producer's risk jumps from 0.02 to about 0.16.

Designing a plan from two points

The usual design brief is the two points from the previous section. The plan's OC curve must pass at or above the producer's point and at or below the consumer's point.

\sum_{d=0}^{c}\binom{n}{d}p_1^{d}(1-p_1)^{n-d} \ge 1-\alpha \qquad \sum_{d=0}^{c}\binom{n}{d}p_2^{d}(1-p_2)^{n-d} \le \beta

Here p_1 is the AQL and p_2 the LTPD. The handbook notes that these equations have no direct solution, so plans are found by search. A simple search tries c = 0, 1, 2, \dots in turn. For each c it finds the smallest n that protects the consumer, then checks whether that plan also protects the producer. The first c that passes both tests gives the smallest plan.

The other route to a plan is a published table. Section 9.7 outlines MIL-STD-105D, which indexes its plans by the AQL and the lot size.

Rectifying inspection: AOQ, AOQL and ATI

A rejected lot is often not sent back. Instead it is inspected 100%, and every defective found is replaced with a good item. This is rectifying inspection. Accepted lots ship with whatever defectives they contained outside the sample, and rejected lots ship clean. Over many lots of the same incoming quality p, the fraction defective in what ships is the average outgoing quality.

\text{AOQ} = \frac{P_a\, p\, (N - n)}{N}

Here N is the lot size. When N is much larger than n, AOQ is close to P_a\,p. At p = 0 there is nothing to find, so AOQ is zero. When incoming quality is very bad, nearly every lot is rejected and screened, so AOQ falls back toward zero. In between it peaks. That peak is the average outgoing quality limit (AOQL), the worst long-run outgoing quality the plan can deliver, whatever the supplier sends.

The price of that guarantee is inspection. The average total inspection per lot counts the sample in every lot plus the rest of every rejected lot.

\text{ATI} = n + (1 - P_a)(N - n)
Handbook 6.2.3.2

For the plan n = 52, c = 3 with N = 10{,}000, the handbook finds an AOQL of about 3.72%, reached when incoming lots are close to 6% defective. Its text prints the value as .372, a slipped decimal point; its own table shows 0.0372.

Lots of N = 10{,}000 arrive with 3% defective. The plan n = 52, c = 3 accepts such a lot with probability P_a = 0.9295, and rejected lots are screened completely. What is the average total inspection per lot, to the nearest item?

\text{ATI} = 52 + (1 - 0.9295)(10{,}000 - 52) = 52 + 0.0705 \times 9{,}948 \approx 753. Every lot costs 52 inspections, and the 7% of lots that fail cost another 9,948 each. The handbook gets the same 753; rounding P_a to 0.930 first would give 748.

Double, multiple and sequential plans

A single plan always inspects n items, even when the first few make the answer obvious. Plans that sample in stages can stop early, which lowers the average sample number (ASN), the expected number of items inspected per lot.

Double sampling takes up to two samples.

  1. First sample. Inspect n_1 items and count d_1. Accept if d_1 \le c_1. Reject if d_1 > c_2.
  2. Second sample. Otherwise inspect n_2 more items and count d_2. Accept if d_1 + d_2 \le c_2, and reject if not.

The handbook designs double plans from tables indexed by the ratio R = p_2/p_1, built for \alpha = 0.05 and \beta = 0.10. For p_1 = 0.01 and p_2 = 0.05, R = 5, and the nearest row, R = 4.65, gives c_1 = 2 and c_2 = 4. Holding the consumer's point gives n_1 = n_2 = 108. That plan accepts 96% of lots at 1% defective and 9% at 5% defective. The expected sample size of a double plan depends on p.

\text{ASN} = n_1 P_1 + (n_1 + n_2)(1 - P_1) = n_1 + n_2(1 - P_1)

where P_1 is the probability of reaching a decision on the first sample.

The handbook's ASN example uses n_1 = 50, c_1 = 2, n_2 = 100, c_2 = 6 with lots 6% defective. The first sample accepts with probability 0.416 and rejects with probability 0.029. What is the average sample number?

P_1 = 0.416 + 0.029 = 0.445, so \text{ASN} = 50 + 100 \times (1 - 0.445) = 105.5. The handbook writes it as 50(0.445) + 150(0.555) and reports 106.

Multiple sampling extends the idea to as many as k stages; MIL-STD-105D uses seven. After stage i, the cumulative count of defectives is compared with an acceptance number a_i and a rejection number r_i, and sampling continues while the count sits between them. Some plans do not allow acceptance at the earliest stages, though rejection is always possible.

Sequential sampling takes the idea to its limit. It inspects one item at a time and decides after each. Plot the cumulative number of defectives against the number inspected. Two parallel lines split the chart into accept, continue and reject regions.

\begin{aligned} x_a &= -h_1 + s\,n \qquad x_r = h_2 + s\,n \\[6pt] h_1 &= \frac{1}{k}\log\frac{1-\alpha}{\beta} \qquad h_2 = \frac{1}{k}\log\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha} \qquad s = \frac{1}{k}\log\frac{1-p_1}{1-p_2} \\[6pt] k &= \log\frac{p_2(1-p_1)}{p_1(1-p_2)} \end{aligned}

Accept when the count falls to or below x_a, and reject when it reaches or passes x_r. The handbook's example uses p_1 = 0.01, p_2 = 0.10, \alpha = 0.05 and \beta = 0.10, which gives x_a = -0.939 + 0.040n and x_r = 1.205 + 0.040n. Two defectives among the first 19 items reject the lot, and no lot can be accepted until 24 items in a row have passed. Since the walk could in principle go on until the whole lot is inspected, the handbook suggests stopping at three times the single plan's sample size, here 3 \times 52 = 156 items.

Standard schemes and skip-lot sampling

Few organizations design plans from scratch. The most widely used tables began as US Army and Navy procedures in the Second World War and became MIL-STD-105A in 1950. Revision 105D was issued in 1963. The American National Standards Institute adopted it as ANSI Z1.4 in 1971, the International Organization for Standardization adopted it as ISO 2859 in 1974, and the 1989 revision, 105E, is the latest. All three keep the same basic tables.

The standard is a scheme. For each situation it gives a normal, a tightened and a reduced plan, plus rules for switching between them, all built around the AQL. Using it takes seven steps.

  1. Agree on the AQL with the supplier.
  2. Choose the inspection level. Three general and four special levels set how fast the sample grows with the lot size.
  3. Note the lot size.
  4. Find the sample size code letter from the lot size and the inspection level.
  5. Choose single, double or multiple sampling.
  6. Read the plan from the table for that code letter, plan type and AQL.
  7. Start with normal inspection and follow the switching and stopping rules.

The switching rules tie the plan to the supplier's record. Evidence that quality is worse than the AQL, such as two rejected lots among five consecutive ones, moves inspection to the tightened plan. Five consecutive accepted lots on tightened inspection move it back to normal. A sustained run of good lots can earn reduced inspection, and a supplier that stays on tightened inspection can have acceptance stopped altogether.

Skip-lot sampling rewards a proven supplier more directly by inspecting only some lots. The handbook recommends it only for product with a demonstrated record of very good quality.

  1. Design a reference plan, a single sampling plan with the risks you want.
  2. Inspect every lot with the reference plan until i consecutive lots are accepted. The integer i is the clearance number.
  3. Inspect a random fraction f of the lots that follow, with 0 < f < 1.
  4. Return to every lot as soon as an inspected lot is rejected.

If P is the reference plan's probability of acceptance, the skip-lot scheme accepts with probability

P_a(f, i) = \frac{fP + (1-f)P^{\,i}}{f + (1-f)P^{\,i}}

and inspects F = f/\left[(1-f)P^{\,i} + f\right] times as many items as the reference plan. With the handbook plan (52, 3) as reference, f = 0.25 and i = 5, a supplier running at 2% defective is inspected about 14 items per lot instead of 52, and 99.5% of lots pass. The cost shows up at worse quality. At 5% defective the scheme accepts 84% of lots, against 74% for the reference plan alone.

A supplier is on skip-lot sampling with f = 0.25 and clearance number i = 5. One of the randomly chosen lots is inspected and rejected. What happens next?

  • Acceptance of the supplier's lots stops until the process is fixed
  • Every lot is inspected again until 5 consecutive lots are accepted
  • Skipping continues at f = 0.25, and only the rejected lot is screened
  • The fraction inspected doubles to f = 0.5

A rejected lot sends the scheme back to normal inspection of every lot with the reference plan. Skipping resumes only after the supplier clears i = 5 consecutive accepted lots again.

Takeaways

  • Acceptance sampling decides the fate of lots from a random sample. It protects the customer and does nothing to improve the process.
  • A single plan (n, c) accepts a lot when the sample holds c or fewer defectives. Its OC curve gives P_a for every lot quality.
  • The AQL and producer's risk α, and the LTPD and consumer's risk β, are two points a plan's OC curve must respect. Finding the plan takes a search, or a table.
  • With rectifying inspection, the AOQL caps the long-run outgoing fraction defective, and the ATI is the inspection effort that pays for it.
  • Double, multiple and sequential plans reach the same protection with fewer items on average. MIL-STD-105D (now ANSI Z1.4 and ISO 2859) and skip-lot sampling adjust inspection to the supplier's record.
