X̄ and R or s charts

Most variables charts in industry track the average of a small subgroup and the spread inside it, side by side. This module shows why averaging helps, how to form subgroups that keep the chart honest, and where each constant in the limit formulas comes from.

Why chart averages

A lathe turns steel shafts to a nominal diameter of 25.000 mm. Every hour an inspector measures a few shafts made one after another. You could plot each shaft on its own. The standard Shewhart chart in the handbook plots the average of each small group instead. The group is a subgroup, and its size is n.

Averages vary less than the values they come from. If single diameters have standard deviation \sigma, the average of n independent diameters has standard deviation

\sigma_{\bar{x}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}

With four shafts per subgroup the averages scatter half as much as single shafts, and the control limits close in by the same factor. A shift in the process mean moves the averages just as far as it moves single shafts, so against the tighter limits it stands out sooner. Averages also follow the normal curve more closely than single values do, so the 0.27% false-alarm rate from module 2 holds up even when the raw measurements are a little skewed.

Single shaft diameters have a standard deviation of 0.010 mm. You chart the average of each subgroup of 4 shafts. What is the standard deviation of the plotted averages?

  • 0.010 mm
  • 0.0025 mm
  • 0.005 mm
  • 0.020 mm

\sigma_{\bar{x}} = \sigma/\sqrt{n} = 0.010/\sqrt{4} = 0.005 mm. Halving the spread of the plotted points halves the distance from the center line to each control limit.

Rational subgroups

An X̄ chart compares two kinds of variation. The spread within each subgroup sets the width of the limits. The differences between subgroup averages are what the chart watches. Shewhart's advice was to form subgroups so that the first kind contains only common-cause variation, leaving any special cause to show up in the second. He called subgroups chosen this way rational.

In practice a rational subgroup is usually a few consecutive units from one machine, one cavity or nozzle, one operator and one lot of material, collected over a span short enough that nothing is likely to change inside it. The time between subgroups then gives special causes room to appear.

The mixed chart shows a pattern called stratification. Points crowd the center line, with far fewer near the limits than chance predicts. Stratification can pass for an unusually steady process. Its cause is that each subgroup mixes streams with different means, so the range measures the gap between streams, and the chart cannot see any change smaller than that gap.

Key idea

Put only common-cause variation inside a subgroup. Whatever you mix into a subgroup becomes part of the noise the chart ignores.

For six weeks, every point on an X̄ chart of fill volume has sat close to the center line, and none has come near a limit. Each subgroup takes one bottle from each of four filling heads. What is the most likely explanation?

  • The process is exceptionally stable, and no action is needed
  • The gauge is too coarse to show real differences
  • The heads have different means, which inflates the ranges and widens the limits
  • The wrong A₂ constant was used for subgroups of four

This is stratification. Differences between heads appear inside every subgroup, so \bar{R} overstates the common-cause spread and the limits are too wide for the averages they enclose. Subgroup by head, or chart each head separately, and the chart regains its sensitivity.

Estimating sigma from subgroups

The limits need the process standard deviation \sigma, which is never known exactly. The handbook gives two ways to estimate it from m subgroups of size n. Both use only the spread within subgroups, which is what makes rational subgrouping matter.

From ranges. The range R of a subgroup is its largest value minus its smallest. For normal data the average range is proportional to \sigma, E(R) = d_2\,\sigma, where d_2 depends only on n. Dividing the average range by d_2 estimates \sigma.

From standard deviations. The sample standard deviation s runs slightly low on average, E(s) = c_4\,\sigma with c_4 < 1. Dividing by c_4 removes the bias.

\hat{\sigma} = \frac{\bar{R}}{d_2} \qquad\text{or}\qquad \hat{\sigma} = \frac{\bar{s}}{c_4}

Neither constant is mysterious. d_2 is the average range of n values drawn from a standard normal distribution, and c_4 is the average of their standard deviation. You can find both by simulation.

The histogram also shows that a single range is a rough estimate. For subgroups of five, nine ranges in ten fall somewhere between about 1 and 4 standard deviations. Averaging the ranges of 25 or more subgroups is what makes \bar{R}/d_2 dependable.

The control limits

Put the estimate of \sigma into Shewhart's recipe for an average, \bar{\bar{x}} \pm 3\hat{\sigma}/\sqrt{n}, and fold the constants into a single factor. With ranges, the X̄ and R charts are

\begin{aligned} \bar{x}\text{ chart:}\quad & \text{UCL} = \bar{\bar{x}} + A_2\bar{R},\quad \text{CL} = \bar{\bar{x}},\quad \text{LCL} = \bar{\bar{x}} - A_2\bar{R}\\ R\text{ chart:}\quad & \text{UCL} = D_4\bar{R},\quad \text{CL} = \bar{R},\quad \text{LCL} = D_3\bar{R} \end{aligned}

where A_2 = 3/(d_2\sqrt{n}). The R chart applies the same three-sigma recipe to the range itself. Its standard deviation is d_3\sigma, which gives D_4 = 1 + 3d_3/d_2 and D_3 = 1 - 3d_3/d_2. For n \le 6 that lower value is negative, and since a range cannot be negative, D_3 is set to zero.

With standard deviations, the X̄ and s charts are

\begin{aligned} \bar{x}\text{ chart:}\quad & \text{UCL} = \bar{\bar{x}} + A_3\bar{s},\quad \text{CL} = \bar{\bar{x}},\quad \text{LCL} = \bar{\bar{x}} - A_3\bar{s}\\ s\text{ chart:}\quad & \text{UCL} = B_4\bar{s},\quad \text{CL} = \bar{s},\quad \text{LCL} = B_3\bar{s} \end{aligned}

where A_3 = 3/(c_4\sqrt{n}) and B_4, B_3 = 1 \pm 3\sqrt{1-c_4^2}/c_4, with B_3 again floored at zero.

Twenty-five subgroups of five shafts give \bar{\bar{x}} = 25.004 mm and \bar{R} = 0.023 mm. Using A_2 = 0.577, what is the upper control limit of the X̄ chart, to three decimals?

\text{UCL} = 25.004 + 0.577 \times 0.023 = 25.004 + 0.0133 = 25.017 mm. The LCL is 25.004 - 0.0133 = 24.991 mm. The R chart's upper limit is D_4\bar{R} = 2.115 \times 0.023 = 0.049 mm, and its lower limit is zero because D_3 = 0 for subgroups of five.

R or s?

Both charts estimate the same \sigma. They differ in precision. The range uses the two most extreme values in a subgroup and ignores the rest; the standard deviation uses every value. For two values the estimates are equivalent. As subgroups grow, the range falls behind.

The handbook gives the efficiency table above and concludes that the range works well for subgroups up to about 10. For larger subgroups it recommends the standard deviation. Typical subgroups have 4 or 5 units, where little is lost by using the range. Read section 6.3.2.1.

The range survives because it is easy to compute by hand on the shop floor and easy to explain. When software does the arithmetic, the s chart costs nothing extra at any subgroup size.

A tablet press is sampled every 30 minutes, and each subgroup weighs 15 tablets. Which chart should track the spread?

  • An s chart, because the range wastes information in subgroups larger than about 10
  • An R chart, because it is the standard choice
  • A moving range chart of the subgroup averages
  • Either one, since they give identical limits

The handbook recommends the range only up to subgroups of about 10. With 15 tablets per subgroup, \bar{s}/c_4 estimates \sigma more precisely, so use the X̄ and s charts.

Running the charts

A few habits make X̄ charts trustworthy.

  1. Read the spread chart first. The X̄ limits are built from \bar{R} or \bar{s}. If the spread is unstable, those limits are unreliable, and an X̄ point beyond them may reflect extra spread rather than a moved mean. Bring the R or s chart into control, then judge the averages.
  2. Collect enough subgroups. Shewhart suggested at least 25 subgroups of size 4, all in control, before concluding that a process is stable. With fewer, the limits depend heavily on which subgroups happened to be drawn, and the textbook false-alarm rates, which assume known parameters, no longer hold.
  3. Recompute the limits only for a reason. The handbook lists three: at least 30 new points with no known process change, which sharpen the estimate of spread; a major change to the process; and the removal of data from a preventable disruption, such as a power outage or a bad lot of consumables. Recomputing on a schedule lets the limits follow the process and hides slow changes.
  4. Expect the occasional false alarm. For a stable normal process each subgroup has a 0.27% chance of plotting outside three-sigma limits, so the in-control average run length is 1/0.0027 \approx 370 subgroups. At one subgroup an hour, around the clock, that is a false alarm about every two weeks. Module 2 explains run lengths in detail.

On the same set of 25 subgroups, the X̄ chart has one point above its UCL and the R chart has two points above its UCL. What should you do first?

  • Look for what moved the process mean at the X̄ signal
  • Widen the X̄ limits until the point fits
  • Investigate the R chart signals, because the X̄ limits depend on a stable R̄
  • Ignore both, since 3 signals out of 50 points is normal

The spread chart comes first. If special causes are inflating some ranges, \bar{R} is too large and the X̄ limits too wide, or the X̄ signal itself may come from extra spread in that subgroup. Find and remove the causes behind the R signals, recompute, and then judge the X̄ chart.

An X̄ and R chart has run in Phase II for three months with frozen limits. Which of these is a sound reason to recompute the limits?

  • Yesterday a point fell outside the UCL
  • The limits look wide compared with the specification
  • It is the first day of a new quarter
  • The machine received a new spindle and has since run 30 subgroups with no signals

A major process change, here a new spindle, is a valid reason, and 30 or more new in-control points give a sound basis for the new limits. Signals, specifications and calendars are not reasons to move control limits.

Takeaways

  • Averages of n values vary with standard deviation \sigma/\sqrt{n}, so an X̄ chart sees a shift in the mean sooner than a chart of single values.
  • Form rational subgroups, with only common-cause variation inside each one, so that special causes appear between subgroups. Mixing streams inflates the limits and hides shifts.
  • Estimate \sigma from the spread within subgroups, as \bar{R}/d_2 or \bar{s}/c_4. The factors A_2, D_3, D_4 and A_3, B_3, B_4 turn that estimate into three-sigma limits.
  • Use the range for subgroups up to about 10 and the standard deviation for larger ones.
  • Read the R or s chart before the X̄ chart, base the limits on at least 25 subgroups, and recompute them only for a good reason.
