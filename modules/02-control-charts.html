How control charts work

A control chart is a running hypothesis test. Each new point asks whether the process still looks like it did when the limits were set. This module explains where the limits come from, what they cost, and how to read the patterns between them.

Anatomy of a control chart

A control chart plots one statistic of the process in time order: a single measurement, the average of a small sample, a count of defects. Three horizontal lines turn that plot into a decision tool.

  • The center line is the average of the statistic while the process is in control.
  • The upper and lower control limits (UCL and LCL) sit far enough from the center line that almost every in-control point falls between them.
  • The bands between the center line and the limits are often split into zones at one, two and three standard deviations. They come into play with the run rules later in this module.

For a statistic w with in-control mean \mu_w and standard deviation \sigma_w, Shewhart's general model is

\text{UCL} = \mu_w + k\,\sigma_w \qquad \text{CL} = \mu_w \qquad \text{LCL} = \mu_w - k\,\sigma_w

with k = 3 almost everywhere. Every chart in this course is a version of this recipe. They differ in which statistic they plot and how they estimate \mu_w and \sigma_w.

Handbook 6.3.1

Points inside the limits do not prove the process is in control. The handbook's example is a run of 25 points above the center line out of 30, followed by 5 below. No single point is extreme, yet the sequence is far too orderly to be chance.

Why three sigma?

Every time a point is plotted, the chart makes a decision, and it can be wrong in two ways.

False alarm

Type I error, probability α

A point lands outside the limits even though nothing changed. Someone stops the line and searches for a cause that does not exist.

Missed signal

Type II error, probability β

The process has shifted, but the point still lands inside the limits. Bad product keeps flowing until a later point catches it.

Wide limits make false alarms rare and misses common. Narrow limits do the opposite. For normally distributed data, limits at three standard deviations leave 0.135% in each tail, a false-alarm rate of 0.27% per point. That is close to the "0.001 probability limits" the handbook describes, which put exactly 0.1% in each tail at \pm 3.09\sigma. British practice tends to use probability limits; American practice uses three sigma whether or not the data are exactly normal.

Three sigma is a convention, and the handbook calls the underlying choice arbitrary: management can pick the false-alarm risk it can live with. The convention has held for a century because it balances the two errors well for most processes, and because it is robust. Even when the data are not normal, three-sigma limits usually keep false alarms rare.

Common mistake

Skewed data shift the risk to one side. The handbook gives a Poisson example with mean 0.8: the three-sigma upper limit is 3.48, and the chance of exceeding it is about 0.009, nearly seven times the nominal 0.00135. Module 5 shows how to handle this for counts.

A three-sigma chart monitors a normally distributed statistic that has not changed. On average, how often will a point fall outside the limits?

  • About 1 point in 22
  • About 1 point in 100
  • About 1 point in 370
  • Never, if the process is in control

The two tails beyond ±3σ hold 0.135% each, 0.27% in total. One in 1/0.0027 ≈ 370 points is a false alarm.

Average run length

A false-alarm rate per point is hard to feel. The average run length (ARL) turns it into something operational: the average number of points plotted before the chart signals. If each point independently signals with probability p, the wait until the first signal follows a geometric distribution with mean

\text{ARL} = \frac{1}{p}

Two ARLs describe any chart. The in-control ARL, written \text{ARL}_0, should be long: with three-sigma limits it is 1/0.0027 \approx 370. The out-of-control ARL for a given shift should be short. For a shift of one standard deviation, the chance a point lands beyond the upper limit is P(Z > 2) \approx 0.0228, so a three-sigma chart takes about 44 points on average to notice.

A three-sigma chart's process mean shifts up by exactly one standard deviation. Each new point now has about a 2.28% chance of landing beyond a limit. What is the average run length, in points, until the chart signals? Round to the nearest whole number.

ARL = 1/p = 1/0.0228 ≈ 44 points. The handbook gives 44.0 for this case. Small shifts take a Shewhart chart a long time to notice, which is the motivation for the CUSUM and EWMA charts in modules 6 and 7.

Run rules

A point beyond three sigma is the bluntest signal. Patterns inside the limits carry information too, and in 1956 the Western Electric Company's quality handbook codified a set of rules to catch them. They are known as the WECO rules, and the NIST handbook lists four, plus two trend rules.

Each rule is tuned to have a false-alarm probability similar to a single point beyond three sigma. Used together, though, they add up. The handbook notes that the in-control ARL falls from 371 with the three-sigma rule alone to about 92 with all four WECO rules. More sensitivity to real shifts comes at the price of roughly four times as many false alarms, so many sites apply the full set only to charts where small shifts matter.

On an X̄ chart, the last five points sit at +1.4σ, +0.6σ, +1.8σ, +1.2σ and +1.1σ above the center line. Which rule, if any, do they violate?

  • Rule 1: one point beyond 3σ
  • Rule 2: two of three beyond 2σ
  • Rule 3: four of five beyond 1σ on the same side
  • Rule 4: eight in a row on one side

Four of the five points (+1.4, +1.8, +1.2, +1.1) are beyond +1σ on the same side, which completes rule 3. It is the signature of a moderate upward shift of about one standard deviation.

A plant switches on all four Western Electric rules on a chart that previously used only the three-sigma rule. If the process stays in control, what happens?

  • False alarms stay the same, because the process is in control
  • False alarms become roughly four times as frequent
  • False alarms become rarer, because the rules double-check each other

Each rule adds its own small false-alarm probability. The handbook puts the in-control ARL at about 92 points with all four rules, against 371 with the three-sigma rule alone.

Setting limits: Phase I and Phase II

Control limits are only as good as the data they come from. If the baseline includes special causes, the limits will be too wide and the chart will miss the very problems it exists to catch. That is why limit-setting is its own phase.

  1. Collect enough history. Shewhart suggested at least 25 subgroups of size 4 before concluding a process is in control.
  2. Compute trial limits from all of it.
  3. Investigate every signal. If a point has a documented assignable cause, drop it and recompute. If no cause can be found, it usually stays.
  4. Freeze the limits and move to Phase II, plotting new data against them.

Once in Phase II, recalculating limits should be rare and deliberate. The handbook suggests doing it only with a valid reason: at least 30 new points with no known process change, a major change to the process, or the removal of a preventable disruption. Recomputing limits every week lets the chart drift along with the process and hides slow changes.

During Phase I, one point falls outside the trial limits. The lab log shows the sample was tested with a mislabeled calibration standard. What should you do?

  • Keep it; every point is part of the process history
  • Exclude it and recompute the trial limits
  • Widen the limits to four sigma so it fits

Phase I aims for limits that describe the process when only common causes act. A point with a documented special cause does not belong in that baseline, so remove it and recompute. Repeat until no unexplained signals remain.

Takeaways

  • Every control chart plots a statistic against limits at its in-control mean plus or minus three standard deviations.
  • Limits trade false alarms against missed shifts. Three sigma gives a false alarm about once every 370 points for normal data.
  • Average run length summarizes a chart: long when nothing has changed, short when something has. A three-sigma chart needs about 44 points to catch a one-sigma shift.
  • Run rules catch patterns inside the limits, at the cost of more frequent false alarms.
  • Phase I builds limits from a clean baseline; Phase II freezes them and monitors.
