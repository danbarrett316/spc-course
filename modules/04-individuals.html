Individuals and moving range charts

Sometimes a process gives you one number per batch, per day or per test. The individuals chart plots those numbers directly and learns what normal variation looks like from the gap between each value and the one before it.

When a subgroup is a single value

An X̄ chart needs several units made close together in time. Many processes cannot supply them.

  • Destructive tests. Crushing a concrete cylinder or pulling a weld apart destroys the sample, so labs test as few as they can.
  • Slow or batch processes. A reactor that makes one batch per shift yields one assay result per batch. Measuring that batch three times would only repeat the measurement error.
  • One value per period. Monthly energy use per tonne, weekly scrap cost and the daily check of a reference standard each arrive once per period.

For data like these the handbook uses the individuals chart, also called the X chart or I chart, paired with a moving range chart. Together they are often written I-MR or X-mR.

Which of these processes is the best fit for an individuals and moving range chart?

  • A bottling line that fills 600 bottles an hour, with fill volume measured by a scale
  • The number of scratches found on each painted panel
  • Tensile strength of a casting alloy, tested by breaking one bar from each heat
  • The fraction of orders shipped late each week

Each heat of alloy yields one tensile result, and the test destroys the bar. One value per heat is the natural subgroup, so the individuals chart fits. The bottling line could form rational subgroups, and the counts and proportions belong on attributes charts.

Spread from consecutive values

An X̄ chart estimates \sigma from the spread inside each subgroup. A single value has no inside, so the individuals chart uses the difference between consecutive values instead. The moving range at point i is

\text{MR}_i = \lvert x_i - x_{i-1} \rvert

Two values taken one after the other are the closest thing these data have to a rational subgroup of size 2. Their difference reflects short-term variation and little else. The average moving range divided by d_2 for subgroups of two estimates \sigma, and three-sigma limits follow:

\begin{aligned} \text{Individuals:}\quad & \text{UCL} = \bar{x} + 3\,\frac{\overline{\text{MR}}}{1.128},\quad \text{CL} = \bar{x},\quad \text{LCL} = \bar{x} - 3\,\frac{\overline{\text{MR}}}{1.128}\\[4pt] \text{Moving range:}\quad & \text{UCL} = 3.267\,\overline{\text{MR}},\quad \text{CL} = \overline{\text{MR}},\quad \text{LCL} = 0 \end{aligned}
\bar{x}
average of all the individual values
\overline{\text{MR}}
average of the moving ranges, one fewer than the number of values
1.128
d_2 for n = 2: the average distance between two independent standard normal values, 2/\sqrt{\pi}
3.267
D_4 for n = 2, the same factor an R chart uses for subgroups of two

The moving range chart's lower limit is zero because D_3 = 0 for subgroups of two. The handbook also allows a target value in place of \bar{x}, or a known standard in place of \overline{\text{MR}}, when one exists.

Common mistake

Neighboring moving ranges share a value, so they are not independent. A single unusual value creates two large moving ranges in a row, one for the jump into it and one for the jump back out. Before hunting for two causes, find the one point on the individuals chart that both ranges share.

The handbook's flow-rate example

The handbook illustrates the chart with the flow rates of ten consecutive batches. The figure below builds the chart one batch at a time, and then lets you chart your own data the same way.

With all ten batches, \bar{x} = 50.81 and \overline{\text{MR}} = 1.8778, so \hat{\sigma} = 1.8778/1.128 = 1.665 and the limits are 50.81 \pm 3 \times 1.665, from 45.8159 to 55.8041. Every batch falls inside them. Every moving range sits below its upper limit of 3.267 \times 1.8778 = 6.13. The handbook concludes that the process is in control.

The handbook's example uses the first ten batches of a flow-rate series. It reports \bar{x} = 50.81, \overline{\text{MR}} = 1.8778 and limits of 45.8159 and 55.8041. It also suggests computing the limits as \bar{x} \pm 3s/c_4 at the start of Phase 2. Read section 6.3.2.2.

Suppose only the first five batches were available: 49.6, 47.6, 49.9, 51.3 and 47.8. What would the UCL of the individuals chart be, to two decimals? You can check your work by setting the slider to 5.

The average is \bar{x} = 246.2/5 = 49.24. The four moving ranges are 2.0, 2.3, 1.4 and 3.5, so \overline{\text{MR}} = 9.2/4 = 2.30 and \hat{\sigma} = 2.30/1.128 = 2.039. The UCL is 49.24 + 3 \times 2.039 = 55.36. With fewer values the estimate of spread is larger and far less certain, which is why limits from five points should be treated as provisional.

Limits for Phase 2

For routine monitoring, the handbook adds an alternative. It puts the limits at \bar{x} \pm 3s/c_4, where s is the ordinary standard deviation of all the Phase 1 values. Once Phase 1 has shown the process to be stable, every value carries information about \sigma, and s uses that information more fully than the moving ranges do. In simulations of stable normal data, the estimate \overline{\text{MR}}/1.128 has about 1.6 times the variance of s/c_4.

For the flow-rate data, s = 2.034 and c_4 = 0.9727 for ten values, which puts the limits at 44.54 and 57.08. They are wider than the moving-range limits because the last five batches average 52.38 against 49.24 for the first five, and s counts that difference as spread. The next section shows why that matters.

Why moving ranges come first

If s/c_4 is more precise, why does Phase 1 use moving ranges at all? Because Phase 1 data are not yet known to be stable. The standard deviation measures distance from the overall average, so a shift or drift in the baseline inflates it. The moving ranges barely notice, because each one compares a value with its immediate neighbor, and a slow change moves both by nearly the same amount. A step change adds only one large moving range.

Key idea

Use moving ranges to set limits while stability is still in question. Once Phase 1 shows the process is stable, the overall standard deviation gives slightly better limits for Phase 2.

A Phase 1 baseline of 50 daily values contains a slow upward drift that no one has noticed yet. Which estimate of σ stays closest to the true day-to-day variation?

  • The standard deviation of all 50 values
  • The standard deviation divided by c₄
  • The average moving range divided by 1.128
  • The overall range of the 50 values divided by 6

Each moving range compares neighboring days, which a slow drift moves by almost the same amount. \overline{\text{MR}}/1.128 therefore tracks short-term variation, while any estimate built on distance from the overall average absorbs the drift.

Sensitive to skewed data

Averages look more normal than the values they come from, which protects an X̄ chart when the raw data are skewed. An individuals chart has no such protection. Its three-sigma limits assume the individual values are roughly normal, and when they are not, the false-alarm rate drifts away from 0.27%.

Right-skewed data are the common case. Impurity levels, cycle times and particle counts cannot fall below zero but can run high. Their long upper tail crosses the UCL far more often than the 0.135% that normal theory allows, while the LCL can fall below zero, where nothing can reach it.

When a histogram of the Phase 1 data is clearly skewed, two remedies work well. Chart a transformation that makes the data closer to normal; the logarithm often suits impurities and cycle times. Or, when the process allows it, collect small subgroups and use an X̄ chart, whose averages are closer to normal.

Cycle times on a packaging line are strongly right-skewed. What should you expect from an individuals chart with ordinary three-sigma limits?

  • About 0.27% false alarms, split evenly above and below
  • More false alarms than 0.27%, nearly all above the UCL, and an LCL that may be useless
  • More false alarms, nearly all below the LCL
  • No change, because the moving ranges adjust for the skew

The long right tail produces frequent points above the UCL, while the short left tail rarely reaches the LCL, which may even sit below zero. In the figure, moderate skew roughly triples the false-alarm rate and strong skew raises it about sevenfold. Transform the data or chart subgroup averages instead.

Slow to catch small shifts

An individuals chart judges single values, so its limits sit at \pm 3\sigma instead of \pm 3\sigma/\sqrt{n}. A one-sigma shift moves each point only a third of the way to a limit, and the chart needs about 44 points on average to notice, as module 2 showed. An X̄ chart with subgroups of four sees the same shift as two standard errors and needs about 6 subgroups.

Slow detection of small shifts is the individuals chart's main weakness. When one value per period is all you can get and small shifts matter, the CUSUM and EWMA charts in module 6 and module 7 accumulate evidence across many points and catch a one-sigma shift far sooner.

The mean of a process charted with an individuals chart shifts by one standard deviation and stays there. On average, about how many points pass before the chart signals?

  • About 6
  • About 370
  • About 2
  • About 44

After a one-sigma shift, each point lands beyond the UCL with probability P(Z > 2) \approx 0.0228, and almost never below the LCL. The average run length is 1/0.0228 \approx 44 points.

Takeaways

  • Use an individuals chart when the natural subgroup is a single value, as with destructive tests, slow batch processes and figures reported once per period.
  • The moving range \lvert x_i - x_{i-1}\rvert measures short-term variation. The limits are \bar{x} \pm 3\,\overline{\text{MR}}/1.128, and the moving range chart's upper limit is 3.267\,\overline{\text{MR}}.
  • In the handbook's flow-rate example the limits are 45.8159 and 55.8041, and all ten batches are in control.
  • Moving ranges resist shifts and drifts in Phase 1 data. Once the process is shown to be stable, \bar{x} \pm 3s/c_4 gives more precise limits for Phase 2.
  • Individuals charts assume roughly normal data and are slow to catch small shifts. Transform skewed data, and turn to CUSUM or EWMA when small shifts matter.
