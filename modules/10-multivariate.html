Multivariate control charts

A tablet can have an ordinary weight and an ordinary thickness and still be wrong, because heavy tablets should also be thick. When measurements move together, you have to chart them together. This module builds one statistic, Hotelling's T², that watches several correlated measurements at once, then adds two refinements, principal components and the multivariate EWMA.

Two charts are not enough

Most products are measured on more than one characteristic. A tablet press samples tablets for weight and thickness. A machining cell measures a bore's diameter at the top and at the bottom. A wafer gets a thickness and two displacement readings. The handbook notes that most data are naturally multivariate.

The obvious approach is a separate Shewhart chart for each measurement. On a tablet press with a 2 mg standard deviation in weight and 0.04 mm in thickness, that means a weight chart with limits at 250 ± 6 mg and a thickness chart with limits at 4.00 ± 0.12 mm. A tablet passes when it is inside both sets of limits, so together the two charts accept a rectangle.

The trouble is that the two measurements are related. More powder in the die makes a tablet both heavier and thicker, so in-control tablets do not fill the rectangle. They cluster in a tilted ellipse. A tablet that is heavy and thin is unusual for this press, because it suggests the punches squeezed it harder than normal. Yet each of its measurements sits comfortably inside its own limits.

The rectangle makes two kinds of mistake. It misses points in the empty corners across the ellipse, like the heavy, thin tablet. It can also flag points near its other two corners that lie along the ellipse and are ordinary for this process. Separate charts also pile up false alarms. Two charts at 0.27% each give an overall rate near 0.5%, and the exact rate depends on a correlation that neither chart shows.

Key idea

Correlated measurements need one statistic that knows how they vary together. Its limit is an ellipse shaped by the covariance, and it judges each point by its distance from the center in units of the process's own spread.

Weight and thickness have correlation 0.8. A tablet weighs 253 mg (1.5σ above the mean) and is 3.94 mm thick (1.5σ below the mean). What do two separate three-sigma charts and a T² chart say?

  • Both methods signal, because the tablet is unusual
  • Neither signals, because both values are inside ±3σ
  • The separate charts stay quiet and the T² chart signals
  • Only the weight chart signals

The rectangle checks each coordinate alone, and both are within ±3σ. With correlation 0.8 this tablet has T² = 22.5, about twice the limit of 10.7. Drag the test tablet in the figure to 253 mg and 3.94 mm to see it.

Hotelling's T²

To build that statistic, first summarize the process with two objects, as the handbook's tutorial does (section 6.5.4.1). The mean vector holds the average of each variable. The covariance matrix holds every variance and covariance.

The handbook's example measures the length, width and height of five objects. The averages are 4.10, 2.08 and 0.604, and the covariance matrix is

\bar{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 4.10 \\ 2.08 \\ 0.604 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.025 & 0.0075 & 0.00175 \\ 0.0075 & 0.0070 & 0.00135 \\ 0.00175 & 0.00135 & 0.00043 \end{bmatrix}

The diagonal holds the three variances, so 0.025 is the variance of length. Each off-diagonal entry is a covariance, the average product of two variables' deviations from their means:

s_{jk} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_{ij}-\bar{x}_j)(x_{ik}-\bar{x}_k)

The matrix is symmetric, because the covariance of length with width is the covariance of width with length. Dividing a covariance by both standard deviations gives the familiar correlation, r_{jk} = s_{jk}/(s_j s_k).

Use the handbook's covariance matrix above. What is the correlation between length and width? Give two decimals.

The covariance of length and width is 0.0075, and their variances are 0.025 and 0.0070. So r = 0.0075/\sqrt{0.025 \times 0.0070} = 0.0075/0.01323 = 0.57. Longer objects in this sample tend to be wider too.

In 1947 Harold Hotelling proposed a statistic that measures how far a sample mean lies from a reference mean in units that respect this covariance. It generalizes Student's t. For one variable, the squared t statistic can be written as

t^2 = \frac{(\bar{x}-\mu_0)^2}{s^2/n} = n\,(\bar{x}-\mu_0)\,(s^2)^{-1}(\bar{x}-\mu_0)

Replace each number with its vector or matrix counterpart and you have Hotelling's statistic for p variables (handbook 6.5.4.3):

T^2 = n\,(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu})'\,\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu})
n
number of items in each subgroup
\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}
vector of differences between the subgroup averages and the reference means
\mathbf{S}^{-1}
inverse of the covariance matrix
{}'
transpose, which writes the column vector as a row

With two variables the inverse is short enough to write out:

\mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} s_1^2 & s_{12} \\ s_{12} & s_2^2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{S}^{-1} = \frac{1}{s_1^2 s_2^2 - s_{12}^2}\begin{bmatrix} s_2^2 & -s_{12} \\ -s_{12} & s_1^2 \end{bmatrix}

Multiply it out for a single item (n = 1) with standardized deviations z_1 and z_2 and correlation \rho, and you get a formula you can work by hand:

T^2 = \frac{z_1^2 - 2\rho\, z_1 z_2 + z_2^2}{1-\rho^2}

When the variables are uncorrelated, \rho = 0 and T² is the sum of the squared z-scores, as the handbook notes. With positive correlation, the middle term discounts deviations that share a sign and adds to deviations with opposite signs. The tablet that is 1.5σ heavy and 1.5σ thin has T^2 = (2.25 + 3.6 + 2.25)/0.36 = 22.5. All points with the same T² lie on one ellipse, which is the curve in the first figure.

Weight and thickness have correlation 0.8. A tablet is 2.0σ heavier than average and 1.0σ thicker. Compute its T².

T^2 = (2.0^2 - 2 \times 0.8 \times 2.0 \times 1.0 + 1.0^2)/(1 - 0.8^2) = (4 - 3.2 + 1)/0.36 = 5.0. That is well under the limit of about 10.7. A heavy tablet is expected to be thick, so this one is ordinary even though its weight alone sits 2σ from the mean.

Control limits in two phases

A T² chart plots one value per subgroup and compares it with an upper control limit. The handbook does not use three-sigma limits for T². It derives each limit from the distribution of T², which depends on whether the mean and covariance are known or estimated, and on whether the subgroup being judged helped estimate them.

When the mean vector and covariance matrix are known exactly, T² follows a chi-square distribution with p degrees of freedom. The first figure used that case. In practice both are estimated from data and the F distribution takes over. For one sample tested against a fixed mean \boldsymbol{\mu}_0, the handbook gives

T^2 \sim \frac{p(n-1)}{n-p}\,F_{p,\;n-p}

Phase I limits

Take k subgroups of n items. Estimate the mean vector by the grand average \bar{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}, and the covariance matrix \mathbf{S}_p by averaging the k subgroup covariance matrices. Subgroup j then gets T_j^2 = n(\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\bar{\bar{\mathbf{x}}})'\,\mathbf{S}_p^{-1}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(j)}-\bar{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}), compared with this limit from handbook 6.5.4.3.1:

\text{UCL}_{\text{I}} = \frac{knp - kp - np + p}{kn - k - p + 1}\;F_{\alpha;\;p,\;kn-k-p+1}

Here F_{\alpha} is the upper \alpha point of the F distribution. As with any Phase I chart, investigate points above the limit, remove only those whose cause you have found and corrected, and recompute.

Phase II limits

Once the baseline is clean, freeze its estimates and judge each new subgroup against a different limit (handbook 6.5.4.3.2). If a of the original subgroups were removed,

\text{UCL}_{\text{II}} = \frac{p(k-a+1)(n-1)}{(k-a)n - k + a - p + 1}\;F_{\alpha;\;p,\;(k-a)n-k+a-p+1}

The Phase II limit is higher. A future subgroup played no part in the estimates, so its T² carries their full estimation error on top of its own variation. For k = 25 subgroups of n = 4 tablets, p = 2 and \alpha = 0.0027, both formulas use F = 6.41 with 2 and 74 degrees of freedom. The limits come out at 12.48 for Phase I and 13.52 for Phase II.

Individual observations

With one item per sample there is no within-subgroup covariance to pool. The handbook (6.5.4.3.3) estimates the mean and covariance from all m historical observations and uses limits from the beta distribution, due to Tracy, Young and Mason (1992):

\text{LCL} = \frac{(m-1)^2}{m}\,B_{1-\alpha/2;\;p/2,\;(m-p-1)/2}, \qquad \text{UCL} = \frac{(m-1)^2}{m}\,B_{\alpha/2;\;p/2,\;(m-p-1)/2}

where B_{\alpha/2} is the upper \alpha/2 point of the beta distribution. For m = 50 observations on two variables with \alpha = 0.0027, the limits are 0.0028 and 11.77. This chart has a lower limit as well. A T² near zero means every variable sits almost exactly at its mean, and the handbook suggests that can point to a problem with how the data were recorded.

Reading a T² signal

A T² chart condenses everything into one number per subgroup, and that convenience has a price. The handbook (6.3.4.2) names two costs. The scale of T² has nothing to do with the scale of any measured variable, so a value of 18 has no units and no direction. And when T² crosses its limit, the chart does not say which variable, or which relationship between variables, caused it.

The handbook's advice is to run the univariate charts alongside the multivariate one. They keep the physical units in view and help narrow down the culprit. They cannot reveal a change in the correlation itself, so a chart of dispersion belongs in the set as well. For subgrouped data the handbook pairs the T² chart for means with a T_d^2 chart for within-subgroup dispersion, just as an X̄ chart is paired with an R or s chart.

In the tablet example, the X̄ charts show weight creeping up and thickness creeping down by small amounts. Neither movement is alarming on its own. Read next to the T² signal, they point the engineer toward whatever controls compression, such as punch wear or press force.

Common mistake

Treating a quiet T² chart as proof that every variable is fine. T² is least sensitive along the long axis of the ellipse, where the process naturally varies most. A shift that moves weight and thickness up together can stay inside the ellipse while the X̄ charts catch it. Keep both kinds of chart.

The T² chart for a three-variable process signals at subgroup 31. What does that signal tell you on its own?

  • Which variable moved, because T² grows fastest for the variable with the biggest change
  • How far the mean moved, in the units of measurement
  • That the spread within subgroups increased
  • That the subgroup's averages, taken together, are unusually far from the baseline given the usual variances and correlations

A signal says the subgroup is unusual as a whole. To find out why, look at the univariate charts and a dispersion chart, as the handbook recommends.

Principal components

Principal components offer another way to handle correlated variables (handbook 6.3.4.2 and 6.5.5). The idea is to rotate the standardized variables into new variables that are uncorrelated and ordered by how much variance they carry. Each principal component is a weighted sum of the standardized originals. The weights come from an eigenvector of the correlation matrix, and the component's variance is the matching eigenvalue.

Two standardized variables with positive correlation r make the geometry plain. Whatever the value of r, the components point along the diagonals:

\text{PC}_1 = \frac{z_1 + z_2}{\sqrt{2}} \;\text{ with variance } 1 + r, \qquad \text{PC}_2 = \frac{z_1 - z_2}{\sqrt{2}} \;\text{ with variance } 1 - r

For tablets, PC1 measures overall size, so heavy, thick tablets score high on it. PC2 measures the contrast between weight and thickness, so a heavy, thin tablet scores high on PC2. When r = 0.8, PC1 carries 90% of the variance and PC2 only 10%.

Control charts use principal factors. A principal factor is a component divided by the square root of its eigenvalue, so every factor has variance 1 and can be charted with limits at ±3. The handbook lists two advantages. The factors are uncorrelated, and often a few of them capture most of the variation, so you chart fewer series than you have variables. The disadvantage is that the original variables lose their identity, although the leading components sometimes keep a clear meaning, as size and contrast do here.

The handbook works a three-variable example (6.5.5.2) with ten wafers, each measured for thickness, horizontal displacement and vertical displacement. The eigenvalues of their correlation matrix are below. They add up to 3, the number of variables, and their product, 0.499, equals the determinant of the correlation matrix.

Two components carry about 90% of the variation, so charts of two factors watch nearly everything that three separate charts would. Kaiser's rule of thumb, which the handbook mentions, keeps only components with eigenvalues above 1. Here it would keep just the first.

In the handbook's wafer example, what percentage of the total variance does the first principal component explain? Its eigenvalue is 1.769.

Each standardized variable has variance 1, so three variables have total variance 3, which is also the sum of the eigenvalues. The first component explains 1.769/3 = 0.590, or 59.0%.

Multivariate EWMA

A T² chart judges each subgroup on its own, so like a Shewhart chart it is slow to notice a small shift that persists. Module 7 solved that problem for one variable with the exponentially weighted moving average. The multivariate EWMA, or MEWMA, applies the same smoothing to the whole vector of measurements (handbook 6.3.4.3):

\mathbf{Z}_i = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{X}_i + (\mathbf{I} - \boldsymbol{\Lambda})\,\mathbf{Z}_{i-1}
\mathbf{X}_i
the i-th observation vector, measured from the in-control mean
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
a diagonal matrix of smoothing constants \lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_p, each between 0 and 1
\mathbf{Z}_0
the starting vector, the in-control mean (zero once the mean is subtracted)

The chart plots a T² statistic computed on \mathbf{Z}_i instead of \mathbf{X}_i, using the covariance matrix of \mathbf{Z}_i. When every variable gets the same \lambda, Lowry and co-authors (1992) showed that this covariance is a scaled copy of the data's covariance matrix \boldsymbol{\Sigma}:

T_i^2 = \mathbf{Z}_i'\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z_i}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_i, \qquad \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z_i} = \frac{\lambda}{2-\lambda}\left[1-(1-\lambda)^{2i}\right]\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

As i grows, the bracket approaches 1 and the covariance settles at \tfrac{\lambda}{2-\lambda}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}. The handbook points out that software can use the exact form from the first point, which avoids small errors early in the chart.

What chart do you get if you set λ = 1 in the MEWMA?

  • A chart of the average of all observations so far
  • A separate EWMA chart for each variable
  • The T² chart for individual observations
  • A principal factor chart

With \lambda = 1, \mathbf{Z}_i = \mathbf{X}_i and \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{Z_i} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}, so the statistic is \mathbf{X}_i'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{X}_i, the T² of the current observation alone. Slide λ to 1 in the figure and the two charts become identical.

Takeaways

  • Separate charts for correlated measurements accept a rectangle, but the in-control process fills an ellipse. Points that break the correlation slip through.
  • Hotelling's T² measures the distance of a subgroup mean from the baseline in units of the process's own variances and correlations. It is the multivariate version of Student's t.
  • T² limits come from the F and beta distributions, and they differ between Phase I (the baseline) and Phase II (new data).
  • A T² signal says that something changed without saying what. Run univariate charts and a dispersion chart alongside it.
  • Principal components turn correlated variables into uncorrelated factors, often fewer than the original variables, at the cost of their original meaning.
  • The MEWMA smooths the observation vectors before computing T², which makes it much faster at catching small, persistent shifts.
