Case study: a lithography process

Wafers move through a lithography step in cassettes, and every line-width reading carries the marks of its cassette, its wafer and its site. This capstone follows the handbook's lithography study from the first plots to a set of charts and an action plan that fit a batch process.

The process and the data

Lithography prints a circuit pattern onto a silicon wafer. The wafer is coated with a light-sensitive resist, exposed to light through a patterned mask, and developed, which leaves lines of resist that later steps etch into the layers below. The width of those lines matters because the printed pattern becomes the circuit. Lines that come out too wide or too narrow change how the finished device behaves.

Wafers travel in batches. A cassette carries a batch of about 25 wafers through each step, and every wafer in it sees the same recipe, the same chemicals and the same moment in the tool's day. The handbook's study measured line width on 3 wafers from each of 30 cassettes, at 5 sites on each wafer, for 450 readings in all. The handbook calls the sites Top, Lef, Cen, Rgt and Bot.

That nesting is the whole story of this case. A reading from the Top site of wafer 2 in cassette 14 shares its cassette's conditions with 14 other readings and its wafer's conditions with 4 others. Shewhart's charts assume that each reading scatters around the process mean because of one layer of common-cause noise. Here there are three layers, stacked.

Look before you chart

Before it computes a single control limit, the handbook plots the data. It starts with the readings in the order they were measured, then breaks them out by cassette, by wafer and by site. Each plot asks whether that factor moves the line width.

The run plot shows what the handbook found in the real data. Neither the level nor the spread is constant over time, and neighbouring readings resemble each other. That resemblance is expected, because readings arrive in blocks of 5 from a wafer and 15 from a cassette, and neighbours share conditions.

Box plots make the comparison one factor at a time. Each box covers the middle half of a group's readings and the line inside it marks the median. The whiskers reach the most extreme readings within one and a half box lengths, and hollow dots mark anything beyond.

The handbook reads its real plots the same way. Cassette medians range from about 1.7 to 4, the Center site has a lower median than the others, and the three wafer positions look alike, so the handbook treats wafer position as homogeneous. Wafer position has no systematic effect. Wafers in the same cassette still differ from each other at random, and section 12.4 measures by how much.

Judging by the box plots, which factor moves the line width the most?

  • The site on the wafer
  • The wafer's position in the cassette
  • The cassette
  • None of them, since the differences look like noise

Cassette medians run from about 1.6 to 3.8 in the simulated data, and from about 1.7 to 4 in the handbook's. Whole lots run high or low, and that lot-to-lot movement is the largest effect in the data. Any chart for this process has to deal with it.

A standard chart, and why it fails

A quality engineer's first instinct is to treat each wafer as a subgroup of five and draw an X̄ and s chart (module 3). The handbook tries that and two other subgroupings: each site reading on its own, charted as individuals with moving ranges (module 4), and each cassette as a subgroup of 15.

The handbook's charts of the real data look the same, with many points beyond the limits on every mean chart and no signals on the standard deviation charts. A special cause in every second cassette is not believable. The chart is the problem.

The limits on an X̄ chart come from the variation inside the subgroups. For a wafer subgroup, s measures how much the five sites on one wafer differ from each other, so the limits allow for site-to-site variation only. The wafer means themselves move for three reasons: their cassette, their wafer, and the average of five site deviations. With the handbook's variance components,

\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(\bar{x}_{\text{wafer}}) &= \sigma_C^2 + \sigma_W^2 + \frac{\sigma_S^2}{5} = 0.2645 + 0.0500 + 0.0351 = 0.3496 \\ \text{variance the limits assume} &= \frac{\sigma_S^2}{5} = 0.0351 \end{aligned}

The wafer means vary about ten times as much as the limits expect, so their standard deviation is about 3.2 times larger. The limits at \pm 3\sigma_S/\sqrt{5} = \pm 0.56 sit less than one true standard deviation (0.59) from the center line, and about a third of perfectly ordinary wafer means land outside them.

Key idea

An X̄ chart judges subgroup means against the variation inside the subgroups. When a layer of normal variation sits between subgroups, as cassette-to-cassette variation does in a batch process, the limits leave it out and the chart flags ordinary subgroups. The handbook points out that setting limits this way suits continuous processing and causes trouble with batches.

On the X̄ chart with wafer subgroups, a third of the wafer means fall outside the limits, yet the s chart is quiet. What is the best explanation?

  • Most cassettes had a special cause
  • The limits come from site-to-site variation within a wafer, which leaves out the cassette and wafer variation that moves every wafer mean
  • Subgroups of five are too small
  • The line widths are not normally distributed

The s chart is quiet because the spread inside each wafer is stable. The X̄ chart fails because its limits are built from that same within-wafer spread, while the wafer means also carry cassette and wafer variation. The plotted points vary about three times more widely than the limits allow for.

Three sources of variation

To decide what the limits should include, the engineer needs to know how much variation each layer adds. The handbook gets this from a nested random-effects analysis of variance. Nested means each wafer belongs to one cassette only, so wafer 1 of cassette 3 has nothing to do with wafer 1 of cassette 7. Random effects means the 30 cassettes are treated as a sample of all the cassettes the line will ever run, and the analysis estimates how much cassettes vary in general.

y_{ijk} = \mu + C_i + W_{j(i)} + S_{k(ij)}
y_{ijk}
line width at site k of wafer j in cassette i
C_i
cassette effect, with variance \sigma_C^2
W_{j(i)}
effect of wafer j within cassette i, with variance \sigma_W^2
S_{k(ij)}
site-to-site deviation within a wafer, with variance \sigma_S^2 (this includes the fixed site pattern)

Each mean square in the analysis mixes several components, because a cassette mean also carries the wafer and site variation of the 15 readings inside it. The expected mean squares spell out the mixture:

\begin{aligned} \text{MS}_{\text{cassette}} &= 15\,\sigma_C^2 + 5\,\sigma_W^2 + \sigma_S^2 \\ \text{MS}_{\text{wafer}} &= 5\,\sigma_W^2 + \sigma_S^2 \\ \text{MS}_{\text{site}} &= \sigma_S^2 \end{aligned}

Solve from the bottom up. With the handbook's mean squares, \hat\sigma_S^2 = 0.1755, then \hat\sigma_W^2 = (0.4253 - 0.1755)/5 = 0.0500, then \hat\sigma_C^2 = (4.3932 - 0.4253)/15 = 0.2645.

Using the handbook's variance components, what percentage of the total variance comes from cassette-to-cassette differences? Give a whole number.

The share is 0.2645 / (0.2645 + 0.0500 + 0.1755) = 0.2645 / 0.4900 = 0.540, or 54%. Wafers contribute 10% and sites 36%. The handbook's worked example reports the same split, so more than half of all the variation in line width comes from differences between cassettes.

The components explain the failed charts. Every limit in section 12.3 was built from the site component, or from site and wafer variation together, while the cassette component, the largest of the three, moved the plotted points. The next figure lets you change the three components and see what happens to a standard chart.

Three lessons come out of the figure. Any between-subgroup component that the limits leave out inflates the false-alarm rate, whichever layer it sits in. While the cassette component is large, bigger subgroups make the chart worse, because they shrink the limits and leave the cassette variation untouched. And a standard chart behaves only when the cassette and wafer components are small next to the site component, which is the situation Shewhart's subgrouping was designed for.

The components matter beyond control charts. A customer receives line widths from every cassette, so a capability study (module 8) has to use the total variance from all three components. A study that used only the within-wafer spread would overstate what the process can deliver.

Suppose an engineering change removed cassette-to-cassette variation entirely, leaving the wafer (0.05) and site (0.1755) components. Set the figure to match. What happens to the standard chart with wafer subgroups?

  • It fits, and false alarms drop to the nominal 0.27%
  • False alarms rise above the current 34%
  • The s chart starts to signal instead
  • False alarms fall to about 5% per wafer, still about 20 times the nominal rate

With \sigma_C^2 = 0, the wafer means vary with variance 0.0500 + 0.1755/5 = 0.0851, a standard deviation of 0.292. The limits sit at 3\sqrt{0.1755}/\sqrt{5} = 0.562, which is only 1.93 of those standard deviations, so about 5.4% of ordinary wafer means fall outside. The wafer-to-wafer component on its own is enough to break the chart.

Charts that fit the process

The handbook's answer, which it describes as a common solution, is a set of charts, each aimed at one layer of the process.

Lot means with LL limits

Watches lot-to-lot variation

Plot each cassette's mean, with limits set from how much lot means vary from one lot to the next. The handbook labels these limits UCL: LL and LCL: LL.

Standard deviation within lots

Watches spread inside a lot

Plot the s of the 15 readings in each lot against the usual s chart limits. It reacts when one wafer or one site pulls away from the rest of its lot.

Individuals and moving ranges

Watches single readings

Plot every site reading as it comes off the measurement tool. The moving range chart catches a reading that jumps away from its neighbours.

The handbook does not print a formula for the LL limits. A common way to build them treats each lot mean as a single measurement and draws an individuals chart of the 30 lot means, with the spread estimated from the moving ranges between consecutive lots:

\begin{aligned} \overline{MR} &= \frac{1}{29}\sum_{i=2}^{30} \left|\bar{x}_i - \bar{x}_{i-1}\right| \\ \text{UCL: LL},\ \text{LCL: LL} &= \bar{\bar{x}} \pm 3\,\frac{\overline{MR}}{1.128} = \bar{\bar{x}} \pm 2.66\,\overline{MR} \end{aligned}

Consecutive lot means differ by the full lot-to-lot variation, so these limits include it. For the simulated data, \bar{\bar{x}} = 2.52 and \overline{MR} = 0.650, which puts the LL limits at 0.79 and 4.25. The within-lot limits from the X̄–s chart, \bar{\bar{x}} \pm A_3\bar{s} = 2.52 \pm 0.789 \times 0.455, sit at 2.17 and 2.88, about a fifth as wide.

Each chart now has a job, and a signal tells the engineer which layer of the process to look at. In the handbook's real data the LL chart is quiet apart from one lot mean that sits right at the edge of its limits.

The individuals chart needs care. Its limits come from the moving ranges between neighbouring readings, which are mostly differences between sites on the same wafer, so it inherits the narrow-limits problem whenever a lot runs high or low. Read it beside the lot chart. A reading outside the individuals limits in a lot whose mean is inside the LL limits reflects an ordinary lot offset. The moving range chart is the more useful half of the pair here, because a jump between neighbours is rare unless one reading is wrong or a new lot has started.

Common mistake

Widening the limits on every chart. The LL limits belong to the lot means chart only. The s chart and the moving range chart keep their usual limits, because they watch variation inside a lot, and widening them would hide the bad wafer and the bad reading in the figure above.

A new lot's mean plots above the within-lot UCL of 2.88 but well inside the UCL: LL of 4.25. What should the engineer do?

  • Record it and carry on, because lot means this far from center are part of normal lot-to-lot variation
  • Hold the lot and start the out-of-control action plan
  • Adjust the exposure dose to pull the next lot back to center
  • Recompute the within-lot limits so they include this lot

The LL limits describe how far lot means travel when only common causes act, and a lot mean between 2.88 and 4.25 is well within that range. The within-lot limits were never the right yardstick for lot means, so crossing them carries no information.

Write the action plan

A signal is worth something only if it leads to the right action. Module 1 introduced the out-of-control action plan (OCAP), a flowchart written before any signal arrives that says what to check and in what order. For this process the chart set does half the work, because each chart points at a different layer. The plan below is one a quality engineer might write for this step. The handbook describes the charts and leaves the plan to the reader.

Lithography gives early action a payoff. Until the pattern is etched, a lot with bad line widths can usually be reworked. The resist is stripped, and the wafers are coated, exposed and developed again. A problem caught at this step costs a rework. The same problem found after etch can cost the wafers.

Common mistake

Adjusting the exposure dose every time a lot mean crosses the within-lot limits. Those limits flag about half of all ordinary lots, so the adjustments would chase lot-to-lot noise and add variation, which is the tampering of module 1.

The s chart signals for a new lot. Its lot mean sits inside the LL limits and the moving range chart is quiet. After confirming the measurement, where should the investigation start?

  • The lot's exposure recipe and reticle
  • The single highest reading in the lot
  • The lot's readings by wafer and by site, to find which wafer or site carries the extra spread
  • Nowhere, because the lot mean is in control

An s signal with a quiet lot chart and a quiet moving range chart points inside the lot. The usual culprit is one wafer that differs from its lot mates, as in the planted bad wafer, or one site that drifted on every wafer. Breaking the readings out by wafer and by site finds it quickly.

Takeaways

  • Batch processing stacks variation in layers. In the lithography study they are cassette, wafer within cassette, and site within wafer, while a Shewhart chart assumes a single layer.
  • Plot the data by factor before choosing charts. Box plots by cassette, wafer and site showed the structure before any limit was computed.
  • X̄ chart limits come from the variation inside subgroups. When normal variation sits between subgroups, the limits are too narrow and the chart flags ordinary lots. Here about half the lot means fall outside within-lot limits.
  • A nested random-effects ANOVA splits the total variance into components: 54% cassette, 10% wafer and 36% site for the handbook's data.
  • The handbook's chart set fits the process: lot means against lot-to-lot (LL) limits, an s chart for spread within lots, and individuals and moving range charts for the site readings.
  • Each chart points at a different layer of the process, so the out-of-control action plan starts from the chart that signaled, after the measurement is confirmed.
