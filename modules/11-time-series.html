Time series and forecasting

Process data arrive in time order, and neighbouring values are often related. This module shows why that matters for control charts, then builds the handbook's forecasting tools: moving averages, exponential smoothing for level, trend and season, and Box–Jenkins models.

Data with a memory

A time series is a sequence of measurements of one variable taken at equally spaced times (handbook 6.4.1). Hourly reactor temperatures, daily fill weights and monthly sales are all time series. The handbook gives two reasons to model one. A model explains the structure that produced the data, and it lets you forecast, monitor, or feed the result back to control the process.

Time order matters for process control because the charts in modules 2 to 7 assume that, while the process is in control, each observation is independent of the last. Many processes break that assumption. A large reactor holds its heat, so a warm hour is usually followed by another warm hour. A tank level, the thickness of successive coils from a rolling mill and the concentration in a continuous blender all carry part of the previous value into the next one. The handbook calls this internal structure, and lists autocorrelation, trend and seasonal variation as its common forms.

Autocorrelation at lag k is the correlation between the series and a copy of itself shifted by k periods:

r_k = \frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^{n} (x_t-\bar{x})(x_{t-k}-\bar{x})}{\sum_{t=1}^{n} (x_t-\bar{x})^2}

The simplest model with memory is the first-order autoregressive model, AR(1). Each value keeps a fraction \phi of the previous value's distance from the mean, plus fresh random noise a_t:

x_t - \mu = \phi\,(x_{t-1}-\mu) + a_t

Its autocorrelation at lag k is \phi^k, so the memory fades geometrically. Now recall the individuals chart from module 4, which estimates σ from the average moving range. When \phi is positive, neighbouring values are close, the moving ranges are small and the estimate comes out too small. It works out to about \sigma_x\sqrt{1-\phi}, where \sigma_x is the true spread of the series. With \phi = 0.8 the limits sit at about \pm 1.34\,\sigma_x instead of \pm 3\,\sigma_x, and about 18% of the points from a perfectly stable process fall outside them. When \phi is negative the estimate is too large, and real shifts hide inside limits that are too wide.

A standard remedy is to model the time structure and chart what the model cannot predict. If the model is right, its residuals are independent, and an ordinary control chart works on them.

A reactor's hourly temperature has lag-1 autocorrelation 0.8. You put an individuals chart on the raw readings, with σ estimated from the moving ranges. What should you expect while nothing is wrong?

  • False alarms on about 0.27% of points, as usual
  • Frequent false alarms, because the moving ranges understate the spread
  • Fewer false alarms than usual, because the readings change smoothly
  • No effect, because autocorrelation only matters for forecasting

The moving-range estimate is about \sigma\sqrt{1-0.8} = 0.45\,\sigma, so the limits sit near ±1.34σ and roughly 18% of in-control readings fall outside them. Model the autocorrelation and chart the residuals instead.

Averages and moving averages

The handbook builds its forecasting tools from the simplest one upward (6.4.2). A warehouse manager records the amounts delivered by 12 suppliers, in thousands of dollars: 9, 8, 9, 12, 9, 12, 11, 7, 13, 9, 11 and 10. Their mean is 10. To judge 10 as an estimate, take each error (actual minus estimate), square it, and average the squares. This mean squared error (MSE) is 36/12 = 3. Any other constant does worse: an estimate of 9 gives an MSE of 4, and 12 gives 7.

The mean weights every past observation equally, by 1/n. That works for a process that scatters around a fixed level and fails for one with a trend, because old values drag the estimate behind.

A single moving average keeps only the last N values:

M_t = \frac{x_t + x_{t-1} + \cdots + x_{t-N+1}}{N}

With N = 3 the first average of the supplier data is (9 + 8 + 9)/3 = 8.667, and the handbook's MSE drops to 2.42. Placed at the last period it covers, a moving average trails the data. A centered moving average is placed at the middle period instead (6.4.2.2). That is easy for odd N. For even N the middle falls between two periods, so the handbook averages each pair of neighbouring moving averages. With N = 4 the supplier data give centered values of 9.5, 10.0 and 10.75 at periods 3, 4 and 5.

Centering removes the lag, but it uses values from after the period it describes. A centered average can smooth a record of the past and cannot forecast. Neither kind of moving average copes well with a strong trend. The handbook mentions double moving averages for that case and then turns to exponential smoothing.

Single exponential smoothing

Exponential smoothing gives recent observations more weight than old ones, with weights that shrink geometrically into the past. The handbook's version of single smoothing (6.4.3.1, following Hunter, 1986) starts with S_2 = y_1 and then updates

S_t = \alpha\,y_{t-1} + (1-\alpha)\,S_{t-1}, \qquad 0 < \alpha \le 1, \quad t \ge 3

Here S_t is the smoothed value for period t. It uses data only up to period t-1, so it serves directly as the forecast of y_t. The EWMA chart in module 7 uses Roberts' form of the same recursion, with y_t in place of y_{t-1}. The weights are identical, and module 7 shows how they decay. Substituting the recursion into itself gives

S_t = \alpha \sum_{i=1}^{t-2} (1-\alpha)^{i-1}\, y_{t-i} + (1-\alpha)^{t-2}\, S_2

A large \alpha forgets quickly and follows the data closely. A small \alpha remembers far back and smooths heavily. The starting value matters as well, and the smaller \alpha is, the longer its influence lasts. Besides S_2 = y_1, the handbook suggests starting at the process target or at the average of the first four or five observations.

To choose \alpha, compute the one-step forecast errors y_t - S_t and pick the value with the smallest MSE. The handbook's example uses 12 observations: 71, 70, 69, 68, 64, 65, 72, 78, 75, 75, 75 and 70. With \alpha = 0.1, the 11 squared errors add up to 208.8 and the MSE is 19.0. The handbook prints 208.94 because it rounds each squared error before adding. For \alpha = 0.5 the handbook reports an MSE of 16.29. With the S_2 = y_1 start used here the value is 16.50. Small differences like this usually come from how the recursion is started.

For this series the MSE peaks near \alpha = 0.12 and then falls steadily all the way to \alpha = 1, where it is 12.64. At \alpha = 1 the forecast for each period is simply the previous observation. That says something about the data. Each value is closer to the one before it than to any long-run average, so the series wanders more than it scatters. That is the positive autocorrelation of the first section, and heavy smoothing throws useful information away. On independent values scattered around a fixed level, the best \alpha would be small.

Forecasting is the same recursion run one step further, S_{t+1} = \alpha\,y_t + (1-\alpha)\,S_t, which rearranges to S_{t+1} = S_t + \alpha\,\varepsilon_t (6.4.3.2). The new forecast is the old one plus a fraction of the last error. Beyond the end of the data there are no new observations, and the handbook's bootstrap keeps feeding in the last one. With \alpha = 0.1, the last observation 70 and its forecast 71.67, the forecasts for periods 13 and 14 are 71.50 and 71.35.

Use the handbook's series (71, 70, 69, 68, …) with \alpha = 0.1 and S_2 = 71. Compute S_5, the forecast for period 5, to two decimals.

S_3 = 0.1(70) + 0.9(71) = 70.90, S_4 = 0.1(69) + 0.9(70.90) = 70.71, and S_5 = 0.1(68) + 0.9(70.71) = 70.44, matching the handbook's table. The actual value at period 5 is 64, so the error is −6.44.

For a series, the MSE of one-step forecasts is lowest when α is close to 1. What does that suggest about the series?

  • It is random noise around a constant mean
  • The smoothing is working well and the series is very stable
  • Each value is best predicted by the one just before it, so the series wanders
  • α should be reduced so that the forecasts are smoother

With α = 1 the forecast is the previous observation. When that wins, consecutive values are strongly related and the level drifts, which is the positive autocorrelation that also misleads ordinary control limits.

Following a trend with double smoothing

Single smoothing has one constant and no idea of slope, so on a trending series it lags behind. The handbook's example (6.4.3.2) is the series 6.4, 5.6, 7.8, 8.8, 11.0, 11.6, 16.7, 15.3, 21.6, 22.4. With \alpha = 0.3, the forecast for period 10 is 15.4 while the actual value is 22.4.

Double exponential smoothing adds a second equation for the trend (6.4.3.3):

\begin{aligned} S_t &= \alpha\,y_t + (1-\alpha)(S_{t-1} + b_{t-1}) \\ b_t &= \gamma\,(S_t - S_{t-1}) + (1-\gamma)\,b_{t-1} \end{aligned}

The first equation updates the level. Adding the previous slope b_{t-1} to the previous level brings it up to date and removes the lag. The second equation smooths the slope with its own constant \gamma. Unlike single smoothing in the handbook's form, the level here uses the current observation, so forecasts come from the latest level and slope (6.4.3.4):

F_{t+m} = S_t + m\,b_t

To start, set S_1 = y_1. For b_1 the handbook suggests y_2 - y_1, the average of the first three differences, or (y_n - y_1)/(n-1). Its trend example uses the average of the first three differences, b_1 = 0.8, with \alpha = 0.3623 and \gamma = 1.0, the values that minimize the MSE of one-step forecasts. Double smoothing then has an MSE of about 3.7, against about 8.9 for single smoothing even at its best constant, \alpha = 0.977. The forecasts differ in kind as well. Single smoothing projects a flat line near the last value, 22.4. Double smoothing continues the trend with 25.8, 28.7, 31.7, 34.6 and 37.6 for periods 11 to 15.

The handbook's double smoothing fit ends at period 10 with level S_{10} = 22.82 and slope b_{10} = 2.96. What is its forecast for period 13?

Period 13 is three periods ahead, so F_{13} = S_{10} + 3\,b_{10} = 22.82 + 3(2.96) = 31.70. The handbook lists 31.7.

Seasons and triple smoothing

Many series repeat a pattern every season. Retail sales peak before the holidays, and energy use rises every winter. Holt–Winters smoothing (6.4.3.5) adds a third equation for a seasonal index I_t, with L periods per season:

\begin{aligned} S_t &= \alpha\,\frac{y_t}{I_{t-L}} + (1-\alpha)(S_{t-1} + b_{t-1}) \\ b_t &= \gamma\,(S_t - S_{t-1}) + (1-\gamma)\,b_{t-1} \\ I_t &= \beta\,\frac{y_t}{S_t} + (1-\beta)\,I_{t-L} \\ F_{t+m} &= (S_t + m\,b_t)\,I_{t-L+m} \end{aligned}

Dividing by the seasonal index takes the season out before the level is updated, and multiplying by it puts the season back into the forecast. In the handbook's notation \beta smooths the seasonal indices and \gamma smooths the trend. C. C. Holt proposed exponential smoothing in 1957 and extended it to trends in 1958, and Winters added seasons in 1965.

Starting the recursion needs at least one full season, and preferably two to estimate the trend. The handbook's example is six years of quarterly sales, 1990 to 1995, so L = 4. The initial trend averages the change from the first year to the second in each quarter:

b_1 = \frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{382-362}{4} + \frac{409-385}{4} + \frac{498-432}{4} + \frac{387-341}{4}\right) = \frac{5 + 6 + 16.5 + 11.5}{4} = 9.75

For the seasonal indices, the handbook divides each observation by the average of its year (380, 419, 510.5, 591, 675 and 716.75), then averages those ratios quarter by quarter. That gives starting indices of 0.919, 1.006, 1.159 and 0.915, so the third quarter typically runs about 16% above its year's average.

In 1991 the quarterly sales were 382, 409, 498 and 387, an average of 419. What is the third-quarter ratio y_7/A_2 that goes into the initial Q3 index? Give two decimals.

498/419 = 1.19, so the third quarter of 1991 ran 19% above that year's average. Averaging the six third-quarter ratios, one per year, gives the starting Q3 index of 1.159.

The season is what single and double smoothing miss in these data. With the start-up used here, the best MSE for single smoothing is about 6,900, as the handbook reports. Holt–Winters brings it down to about 850 or 900, depending on the constants, a reduction by a factor of about eight. The exact optimum depends on how the recursion is started, which the handbook leaves to software. The handbook also warns against reading too much into a fitted constant of zero. A fitted trend constant of zero only means that the starting trend was already good enough that updating it did not reduce the MSE. The series can still have a trend.

Box–Jenkins models

Smoothing methods are recipes. Box–Jenkins models, popularized by George Box and Gwilym Jenkins in Time Series Analysis: Forecasting and Control, describe the dependence in a series directly and give a systematic way to choose, fit and check a model (handbook 6.4.4).

The models assume a stationary series, one whose mean, variance and autocorrelation do not change over time. A run-sequence plot usually shows whether that holds. If the series trends, difference it by replacing each value with its change from the previous one, Y_t = Z_t - Z_{t-1}. One difference is usually enough. Fitting and subtracting a straight line also works, and a log or square-root transform can steady a variance that grows with the level. Seasonality shows up as a repeating pattern in a run-sequence or seasonal subseries plot, and as peaks in the autocorrelation at multiples of the period. A seasonal difference or seasonal terms in the model handle it.

Two building blocks describe the dependence. An autoregressive model of order p, AR(p), regresses the current value on the previous p values. A moving average model of order q, MA(q), writes it in terms of the current and previous q random shocks. This moving average is a different idea from the smoothing average of section 11.2. Combining the two gives the ARMA model:

X_t = \delta + \phi_1 X_{t-1} + \cdots + \phi_p X_{t-p} + A_t - \theta_1 A_{t-1} - \cdots - \theta_q A_{t-q}
A_t
white noise, meaning independent random shocks with mean zero and constant variance
\phi_i
autoregressive coefficients
\theta_j
moving-average coefficients
\delta
a constant, equal to (1 - \phi_1 - \cdots - \phi_p)\,\mu where \mu is the process mean

A model fitted to a differenced series is called ARIMA, where the I stands for integrated. Building one runs in three stages.

  1. Identification. Make the series stationary, then compare its sample autocorrelation function (ACF) and partial autocorrelation function (PACF) with the patterns in the table below. The partial autocorrelation at lag k is the correlation that remains after the effects of lags 1 to k-1 are removed. Values inside about \pm 2/\sqrt{n} are consistent with zero.
  2. Estimation. Fit the coefficients with software, by nonlinear least squares or, preferably, maximum likelihood.
  3. Diagnostics. Check that the residuals look like white noise, with no pattern over time, constant spread and no autocorrelation. The Ljung–Box statistic tests many residual autocorrelations at once. If the residuals fail, go back to identification.

For an AR(p) process the PACF drops to zero after lag p, which is how its order is read. Mixed models are hard to identify by eye, and the handbook notes that criteria such as AIC are increasingly used to help. Box–Jenkins fitting also needs a reasonably long series. The handbook cites a minimum of 50 observations, and many analysts want 100.

The handbook's worked example (6.4.4.9) is series F from Box and Jenkins, 70 consecutive yields from a batch chemical process. The series shows no trend or season. Its autocorrelations alternate in sign and die out quickly, −0.390 at lag 1 and 0.304 at lag 2, which points to an autoregressive model. The handbook fits an AR(2) model about the series mean of 51.13, with \phi_1 = -0.320 and \phi_2 = 0.180, and forecasts a yield of 60.64 for batch 71.

So which model is right? The AR(2) residuals are slightly cleaner at lag 2, and its coefficients match the handbook's. Its second coefficient is only about 1.5 standard errors from zero, though (the handbook reports 0.1797 with a standard error of 0.1202), so a simpler AR(1) model would also be defensible. Identification often comes down to judgment like this, backed by the residual checks.

For process control, the payoff is in the residuals. Once a model captures the autocorrelation, its one-step forecast errors are independent, and an individuals, EWMA or CUSUM chart can watch them with the usual false-alarm rates, as the residual chart in the first figure did. A special cause then shows up as a forecast error that the model cannot explain.

A stationary series has a sample ACF with one large spike at lag 1 and values inside ±2/√n at every later lag. Its PACF decays gradually. Which model does the handbook's table suggest?

  • AR(1)
  • A seasonal model
  • A non-stationary series that needs differencing
  • MA(1)

The ACF of an MA(q) process is zero beyond lag q, so a single spike at lag 1 means q = 1. A gradually decaying PACF fits the same picture.

Takeaways

  • Control limits assume independent observations. Positive autocorrelation shrinks moving-range limits and floods a chart with false alarms, so model the time structure and chart the residuals.
  • The mean weights all past data equally. A moving average keeps the last N values; centering it removes the lag but also the ability to forecast.
  • Single exponential smoothing weights the past geometrically. Choose α by minimizing the MSE of one-step forecasts, and remember that the starting value matters.
  • Double smoothing adds a smoothed slope for trends, and Holt–Winters adds seasonal indices for repeating patterns.
  • Box–Jenkins modelling makes the series stationary, identifies AR and MA terms from the ACF and PACF, estimates them with software, and checks that the residuals are white noise.
